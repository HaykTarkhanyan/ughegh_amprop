The idea in one paragraph

«Դոու, թրու, թաֆ, քոֆ, բաու»: the same four letters, five readings (though, through, tough, cough, bough), and that is why English children learn to read twice as slowly as Finnish or Italian ones. But English was once written as spoken: «knight» was read «քնիխտ»; then the sounds changed and the writing did not. Over about three hundred years the long vowels moved (the Great Vowel Shift: «հուս» became «հաուս»), and just then printing arrived and froze the spellings: a photograph of the old pronunciation. Armenian started from the opposite rule (one letter per sound) and its spelling is still close to speech, though not fully. English reform keeps failing, partly because old books would become hard to read; Webster's American "color" is the one success. The takeaway: English spelling is not wrong, it is old, and many of its odd letters show how people spoke five hundred years ago. (After the Fable review: the debt / island beat is cut.)

Facts (checked)
  • Reading speed (Seymour, Aro & Erskine, British Journal of Psychology 94: 143-174, 2003; 13 European orthographies): "The rate of development in English is more than twice as slow as in the shallow orthographies"; word-reading accuracy passed 90% by the end of the first school year everywhere except Portuguese, French, Danish and "particularly, English". Finnish, Italian, Greek and Spanish are among the shallow ones. The English sample was Scottish children. The often quoted "34% of words" was not found in the sources read, so it is not used.
  • Great Vowel Shift: mainly 1400-1700 (Wikipedia; other sources start it in 1350 or end it in the 18th century); the long vowels rose one step and the highest became diphthongs: Middle English bite /biːt/ ("beet"), meet /meːt/ ("mate"), house /huːs/ ("hoose"). Its causes are still debated (Discovery UK and others).
  • knight: the k and the gh (a «խ»-like sound) were pronounced in Middle English. From standard histories of English; the k fell silent in the 17th century.
  • Printing: Caxton set up his press in Westminster in 1476; spelling was standardised before the vowel changes finished, "so English writing froze even as it continued to evolve" (Dictionary.com); Caxton's Low Countries printers brought Dutch forms such as "ghost".
  • Latin respellings: dette -> debt (Latin debitum), doute -> doubt (dubitare); iland -> island, with an s added by a false link to Latin insula, though the word is Old English (Mental Floss; Upward & Davidson, The History of English Spelling, 2011, as cited by Hotta's blog).
  • Webster: Noah Webster's dictionaries (1806, 1828) fixed color, center in American spelling. From general knowledge.
  • Armenian: each sound has its own letter (the History of the Armenian alphabet page: it "precisely reflects the phoneme structure"); exceptions named in the script: initial ե read «յե», initial ո read «վո». Others exist (unwritten ը between consonants), so the script says «գրեթե».
