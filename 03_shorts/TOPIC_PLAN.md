## Chosen on 2026-11-28 (Q39, Q40)

- **C1. The healthiest places are tiny, and so are the sickest** (thinking error). The lowest and highest cancer
  rates are both found in small, rural counties: small numbers swing to extremes (Kahneman's example; the small-schools
  money behind it). Showcase: a map simulated with the same true rate everywhere, where only the small places light
  up at both ends. Local anchor: tiny Armenian villages topping some "record" list. To verify: Kahneman's wording, the
  schools story and its numbers.
- **C2. London's "targeted" bombs** (thinking error). 1944 Londoners saw V-1 hits cluster and suspected spies; Clarke
  (1946) split south London into 576 squares and the counts matched pure chance. Showcase: the real grid filling with
  hits, then the bar chart against the Poisson prediction. To verify: the exact table.
- **C3. Monty Hall: stick or switch** (thinking error). Switching wins 2 out of 3; thousands of letters, many from
  PhDs, told vos Savant she was wrong. Showcase: the viewer picks during a pause, then 1,000 simulated games. To
  verify: letter counts, the Erdős story.
- **C4. A random wheel moves your guess** (thinking error). A wheel rigged to 10 or 65, then "what share of African
  countries are in the UN?": the wheel moved the answers. Showcase: the wheel spin, two crowds' answers sliding apart;
  real-world anchors (price tags in drams). To verify: the 1974 medians, replications.
- **C5. Leap years and 10 lost days** (why is it so). The year is 365.2422 days, not 365.25; by 1582 the calendar had
  slipped 10 days, so 4 October was followed by 15 October. Why 1900 was not a leap year. Local anchor: the Armenian
  Church's 6 January, and Jerusalem's 19 January. To verify: dates of adoption.
- **C6. Why the sky is blue and sunsets red** (why is it so). Air scatters blue several times more than red; at sunset
  light crosses far more air. Local anchor: Ararat turning pink at sunset seen from Yerevan. To verify: ratios, air
  mass at the horizon.

## More thinking errors

- **T1. Birthday paradox.** In a group of 23 people the chance of a shared birthday is above 50%; at 70, almost
  certain. Showcase: a calendar filling with dots, collisions flashing; the viewer guesses first. To verify: none
  (standard maths).
- **T2. Monte Carlo, 1913: black 26 times in a row** (gambler's fallacy). Gamblers lost fortunes betting red because
  "red is due". Showcase: the run of blacks, the money flowing; then the coin has no memory. To verify: the story's
  details and numbers.
- **T3. The hot hand: a thinking error about a thinking error.** Psychologists said streaks in basketball are an
  illusion (1985); in 2018 two economists found a hidden bias in that analysis, and some streak effect came back.
  Showcase: coin-flip sequences and the subtle counting bias. To verify: Gilovich 1985, Miller & Sanjurjo 2018.
- **T4. Storks deliver babies (p = 0.008)** (correlation is not causation). A real paper showed European countries
  with more storks have more births; the hidden factor is land area. Showcase: the scatter plot, then the third
  variable appears. To verify: Matthews 2000.
- **T5. Chocolate helps you lose weight: a deliberate hoax** (multiple comparisons). A journalist ran a tiny trial,
  measured 18 things and one came out "significant"; newspapers ran it. Showcase: 18 dice rolls, one jackpot. To
  verify: Bohannon 2015 details.
- **T6. One in 73 million** (prosecutor's fallacy). Sally Clark was convicted of killing her two babies partly on a
  "1 in 73 million" figure; the maths was wrong twice. Showcase: the squared probability, then the right comparison.
  Sensitive: a real tragedy, needs care. To verify: the case, the RSS letter.
- **T7. Dunning-Kruger is partly a statistical artifact.** Random numbers reproduce the famous graph. Showcase:
  random data drawing the same curve. To verify: Nuhfer et al., and what remains of the effect.
- **T8. Why attractive people seem less kind** (Berkson's paradox). Selection makes two unrelated traits look
  opposed: you only meet people who pass a total bar. Showcase: a scatter with a cut-off line. Local anchor: dating or
  university admissions. To verify: a clean source.
- **T9. Fear of flying after 9/11 killed people on the roads** (availability). Americans drove instead of flying; an
  estimated ~1,600 extra road deaths in a year. Showcase: two risk bars over time. To verify: Gigerenzer 2004.
- **T10. Your 90% sure is about 50% right** (overconfidence). Asked for ranges they are 90% sure of, people catch the
  truth about half the time. Interactive: the viewer gives 3 ranges in pauses. To verify: Alpert & Raiffa, newer data.
- **T11. Same choice, opposite answers** (framing). The "Asian disease" problem: 72% pick the sure option when it is
  framed as lives saved, a minority when framed as lives lost. Interactive. To verify: Tversky & Kahneman 1981
  numbers.
- **T12. The rule you cannot find: 2, 4, 6** (confirmation bias). People test only examples that fit their guess.
  Interactive: the viewer proposes triples in a pause. To verify: Wason 1960.
- **T13. The cobra effect and Hanoi's rats** (Goodhart's law). Paying per dead rat in 1902 Hanoi led people to breed
  rats; the cobra version is less documented. Showcase: the reward counter vs the real rat count. To verify: Michael
  Vann's research; mark the cobra story as a story.
- **T14. 38 witnesses who did nothing? Mostly not true** (a famous psychology story re-checked). The Kitty Genovese
  account behind the bystander effect was exaggerated; the newspaper itself corrected it. What the bystander research
  still shows. To verify: NYT 2016, Philpot et al. 2019.
- **T15. The marshmallow test, re-run.** A larger replication found the effect much smaller once family background
  is taken into account. Showcase: two kids, one marshmallow, then the hidden variable. To verify: Watts, Duncan & Quan
  2018.
- **T16. Placebo knee surgery.** Patients who got fake surgery improved as much as those with the real one. Showcase:
  two groups, the same recovery line. To verify: Moseley 2002, newer trials.
- **T17. Benford's law: why 1 is the most common first digit.** In real-world numbers about 30% start with 1, only
  about 5% with 9; used to catch fraud. Showcase: bars from real data (populations, river lengths). Also fits "why is
  it so". To verify: examples, the fraud cases.
- **T18. The Texas sharpshooter: drawing the target after shooting.** Cancer clusters and "amazing coincidences"
  (Lincoln and Kennedy) come from looking after the fact. Showcase: bullet holes, then the circle drawn around them. To
  verify: the coincidence list's falsehoods.
- **T19. Sunk cost: the Concorde.** Britain and France kept funding it knowing it would not pay. Everyday version:
  finishing a bad movie because you paid. To verify: the Concorde history.
- **T20. Survivorship, part 2: "they built things better back then".** Only the good old buildings survived; the bad
  ones were torn down. Showcase: a street of 1900 buildings, most fading out. Local anchor: old Yerevan. To verify: a
  source on demolition rates (may be hard).

## More "why is it so"

- **W1. Why A4 is 210 by 297.** Halve it and the shape stays the same (the √2 ratio); A0 is one square metre.
  Showcase: folding, the rectangles nesting. To verify: ISO 216 history.
- **W2. Why a metre is this long, and slightly wrong.** 1790s France measured from Dunkirk to Barcelona; a hidden
  error left the metre about 0.2 mm short. Ties to the minute video. To verify: the Méchain story, the 0.2 mm.
- **W3. Why seasons are not about distance.** Earth is closest to the Sun in early January; seasons come from the tilt.
  Showcase: the tilted globe and the light spreading. To verify: perihelion date.
- **W4. Why the Moon looks bigger at the horizon.** It does not: a photo shows the same size; the brain's distance
  guess fools us. Showcase: two photos overlaid. To verify: the theories still debated.
- **W5. Why ice floats, and why it matters.** Water is densest at 4 °C, so lakes freeze from the top and fish survive.
  Showcase: water molecules, a lake in winter. Local anchor: Sevan in winter. To verify: none (standard physics).
- **W6. Why you cannot tickle yourself.** The brain predicts your own touch; a robot with a delay makes self-tickling
  work again. Showcase: the delay experiment. To verify: Blakemore 1998.
- **W7. Why aeroplane windows are round.** The first jet airliner (Comet, 1954) broke up in the air partly because of
  stress around square-ish openings. Showcase: stress lines gathering at corners. To verify: the Comet inquiry.
- **W8. Why a marathon is 42.195 km.** The 1908 London Olympics fixed the distance to finish in front of the royal
  box. To verify: the story's details (often embellished).
- **W9. Why a rainbow has seven colours.** Newton chose seven to match the musical scale; the spectrum is continuous.
  Local anchor: the Armenian folk counting of rainbow colours, if any. To verify: Newton's Opticks.
- **W10. Why clocks go clockwise.** Sundials in the northern hemisphere cast shadows that move this way. To verify:
  a solid source.
- **W11. Why the Moon always shows us the same face.** Tidal locking slowed its spin until a day equals its month.
  Showcase: the Moon spinning down. To verify: none (standard).
- **W12. Why old photos have no smiles.** Not only long exposures: smiling for portraits was considered foolish. To
  verify: the history of the photographic smile.
- **W13. Why the Leaning Tower of Pisa leans, and did not fall.** Soft soil, centuries of building pauses, and a 2001
  rescue that pulled it back by 45 cm. To verify: the numbers.
- **W14. Why toilets do not spin by hemisphere.** The Coriolis effect is far too weak in a sink; the bowl shape
  decides. Myth-busting. To verify: the MIT and similar experiments.
- **W15. Why time zones exist: railways.** Every town had its own noon until trains needed timetables (1883 in the
  US). Local anchor: Armenia's time zone changes. To verify: Armenian time zone history.
- **W16. Why daylight saving time exists, and does it save energy?** Origins and what studies find (small or none).
  To verify: the studies; Armenia stopped switching (when).
- **W17. Why plugs and voltages differ by country.** Edison vs Westinghouse; why Armenia has 230 V and type C/F. To
  verify: the history.
- **W18. Why "Bluetooth" is called that.** Named after a Viking king; the logo is his initials in runes. Fun, short.
  To verify: the naming story.
- **W19. Why the sea is salty but rivers are not.** Rivers carry salt, evaporation leaves it behind. Local anchor:
  Sevan (fresh) vs the Dead Sea. To verify: none (standard).
- **W20. Why we see in red, green and blue.** Three cone types; screens fool the eye with three colours. Showcase:
  zooming into a phone screen. To verify: none (standard).
