The idea in one paragraph

By the textbook, carrying a backpack on flat ground is zero work: you hold it up and walk forward, and work counts only motion along the force. So why do you sweat, while a suitcase on wheels feels like nothing? Because a body is not a cart: each step you dip and rise, and your leg muscles lift you and the pack again; measured energy cost grows almost in proportion to the load. A wheel removes that: the floor carries the weight, the case does not bob with your steps, and on a smooth floor the pull is a few percent of its weight. But a wheel has one condition, a smooth floor: on steps, sand or cobbles it bounces and sticks, and the load is back in your hands, which is why some Yerevan pavements still favour the backpack. The close: the textbook says zero; your legs disagree.

Facts (checked)
  • Zero work: on level ground at steady speed the vertical holding force is perpendicular to the motion, so the net mechanical work on the load is zero (textbook definition, W = F·d·cos θ).
  • Why it still costs energy: load-carriage studies: metabolic cost and the positive work at the ankle and knee rise roughly linearly with added mass (PMC3922835); "the energy cost of walking with backpack loads increases progressively with increases in weight carried" (US Army review, PubMed 14964502). The script says «գրեթե համամասնորեն».
  • "A few percent": rolling resistance coefficients for hard wheels on smooth floors are of the order of 0.01-0.05 (standard engineering range, not measured for suitcases); on two-wheel cases the hand also carries part of the weight, depending on the tilt. The script says "on a smooth floor"; the visual gives no number.
  • Rough floors: that small wheels bounce and stick on steps, sand and cobbles is everyday experience, not a study; the Yerevan line is a joke and is voiced as one.
Parked: the history half (cut after the review, F216 option a)

Apollo 11, July 1969; Bernard Sadow's wheeled suitcase, conceived 1970, US patent 3,653,474 (1972): flat, four small wheels, pulled on a strap, it tipped and turned badly (Hagley Museum; Wikipedia, Suitcase; Dynomight). Northwest Airlines pilot Robert Plath, 1987, the Rollaboard: upright, two wheels, telescoping handle; sold first to fellow crew (Travelpro; Hagley). Why so late has no settled answer. Fable's lesson for that story, if it comes back: "the idea was the wheels; the invention was the handle" (F218). Left out for good: the "men were too macho" quote and Taleb's use of the story (both unconfirmed).
