The idea in one paragraph

People reached the Moon before suitcases got wheels. Why is a wheeled case so much easier than the same weight on your back? Physics gives an odd answer: work is force times distance along the force; you hold the pack up and walk sideways, so by the textbook you do no work on it on flat ground. Yet you sweat, because a body is not a cart: each step you dip and rise, and your leg muscles lift you and the pack again; measured energy cost grows almost in proportion to the load. Wheels remove that: the floor holds the weight, the case does not bob with your steps, and on a smooth floor the pull is a few percent of its weight. The first wheeled case that sold (early 1970s) lay flat on four small wheels, pulled on a strap like a dog, and tipped over; fifteen years later a pilot stood it upright with two wheels and a long handle. Why so late has no clear answer (porters, carts, short walks, cobbles), and some Yerevan pavements still favour the backpack. The takeaway: the most obvious inventions are obvious only afterwards.

Facts (checked)
  • Moon vs wheels: Apollo 11, July 1969; Bernard Sadow's wheeled suitcase, conceived 1970, US patent 3,653,474 (1972); flat, four small wheels, pulled with a strap; it tipped and turned badly (Hagley Museum; Wikipedia, Suitcase; Dynomight). Earlier wheeled cases and strap-on wheels existed (one source mentions pre-1930 patents), hence "the first wheeled case that caught on", not "the first ever".
  • Plath: Northwest Airlines pilot Robert Plath, 1987, the Rollaboard: upright, two wheels, telescoping handle; sold first to fellow crew (Travelpro, his company; Hagley). Patent dates conflict (1989 / 1991), so none is given.
  • Physics, zero work: on level ground at steady speed the vertical holding force is perpendicular to the motion, so the net mechanical work on the load is zero (textbook definition, W = F·d·cos θ).
  • Why it still costs energy: load-carriage studies: metabolic cost and the positive work at the ankle and knee rise roughly linearly with added mass (PMC3922835); "the energy cost of walking with backpack loads increases progressively with increases in weight carried" (US Army review, PubMed 14964502). The script says «գրեթե համամասնորեն».
  • "A few percent": rolling resistance coefficients for hard wheels on smooth floors are of the order of 0.01-0.05 (standard engineering range, not measured for suitcases); on two-wheel cases the hand also carries part of the weight, depending on the tilt. The script says "on a smooth floor", and the visual does not give a number.
  • Why so late: no source settles it; the script offers the usual guesses as guesses.
  • Left out: the popular "men were too macho to pull luggage" quote (not confirmed in the search); Taleb's use of the story in Antifragile (not confirmed).
