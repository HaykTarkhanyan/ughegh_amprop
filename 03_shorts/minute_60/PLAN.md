## The idea in one paragraph

This clock has ten hours of a hundred minutes. The French Revolution made it, the same revolution that gave the
world the metre and the kilogram. The metre stayed; decimal time did not last two years. Time held out because
sixty is far older, about four thousand years: the Sumerians and Babylonians counted in sixties, nobody knows
exactly why, but you can count to sixty on your two hands (try it: the thumb taps twelve joints, the other hand
counts the twelves). Sixty also divides well: half an hour is thirty minutes, a third is twenty, a quarter fifteen,
while a third of a decimal hour would be 33.333 without end. Greek astronomers took Babylon's sixty for the degree,
then the hour; Latin called the first division "minuta" (small) and the second "secunda" (second), hence the
«մինուտ» and «սեկունդ» we know from Russian; minute hands only came in the late 16th century. The takeaway: France
changed measures and weights but not the clock; sixty divides well, ten comes from our fingers.

## Facts (checked)

- **French decimal time:** 10 hours, 100 minutes, 100 seconds; started November 1793; made optional on 7 April 1795
  (Mental Floss; World History Encyclopedia), so "not even two years". The metric system came from the same
  revolution (standard history).
- **Base 60:** the Babylonians inherited sexagesimal counting from the Sumerians (Scientific American; Live
  Science); why 60 is unknown (Scientific American). The finger-joint count is a hypothesis about how they counted
  (Live Science), shown with «վարկած» on screen and voiced as "you can count to sixty on your hands" (F187).
- **Divisors of 60:** 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30, 60; 100 has no 3.
- **Minutes and seconds:** Greek and Roman scholars kept sexagesimal fractions in astronomy, first for angles
  (Ptolemy); al-Biruni divided the hour sexagesimally around 1000 and Sacrobosco around 1235 (Wikipedia,
  Sexagesimal). Medieval Latin *pars minuta prima* / *pars minuta secunda*, the standard dictionary etymology.
- **Clocks:** "the first mechanical clocks that displayed minutes appeared near the end of the 16th century"
  (Scientific American).
- **Cut after the review:** why 24 hours, the Egyptian star hours and the unequal seasonal hours (F183; this also
  removes the unchecked decan fact, F231), the list of ten divisors (F184), the separate clocks and France beats
  (F186, F188). Old Armenian «րոպէ» (a moment shorter than a minute; Greek origin unconfirmed) stays out.
