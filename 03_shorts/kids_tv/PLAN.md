# Short: Why small kids get glued to CoComelon-style TV - script and plan

Draft for Hayk's review, 2026-11-25 ("watermelon style tv" read as CoComelon-style: fast, bright, sing-along kids'
shows; say if another show was meant). Script first (Q17). Rendered in Remotion (DECISIONS.md #21). Script source:
`beats.hy.json`.

## The idea in one paragraph

Toddlers seem to switch off the world in front of these shows. Reason one: an old reflex, every sudden change (a
cut, a sound, motion) triggers an automatic "what's that?", and these shows are packed with bright colours, big
faces, simple music, constant motion and frequent cuts. Reason two: repetition, which small children love because
predictable things are pleasant and help them learn. But they are not hypnotised: Anderson's experiments (early 1980s)
showed that when the speech was made incomprehensible, children looked away; attention follows understanding, and
very simple songs are fully understood: grabbing plus understandable is the sticky mix. Is it harmful? One 2011
study found worse attention and self-control right after 9 minutes of a fast cartoon, but could not separate pace
from fantasy or measure lasting effects, and these newer shows are barely studied. WHO: no screens under 1, at most
an hour a day at 2-4 (with experts arguing that content matters more than minutes). Tips: slower story shows,
autoplay off, watch together. The child is not lazy; the show just knows how the brain works.

## Facts (checked)

- **No published cut rate for CoComelon:** parenting sites claim a new shot every 1-2, 1-3 or 2-4 seconds, with no
  frame analysis behind any of it. I tried to measure it (yt-dlp + ffmpeg scene detection), but YouTube serves
  made-for-kids videos to anonymous clients only as ~1 fps storyboards (`_learnings/2026-11-25-1240_...`), so the
  script says only "very frequent cuts". The "Distractatron" story (a second screen used to test what pulls toddlers
  away) has no source found and is left out.
- **The orienting reflex** (beat 2): the classic "orienting response" to novel or sudden stimuli; stated generally,
  no study cited.
- **Anderson et al. 1981** (*Child Development*, "The effects of TV program comprehensibility on preschool children's
  visual attention to television"): segments with scrambled scenes or incomprehensible speech (backward or foreign)
  got less visual attention; "comprehension largely determines attention". Also a 1979 study: with toys around,
  5-year-olds looked at the screen about half as much yet understood as much. Competing view (Singer): formal
  features drive attention; a middle view: features lead early, comprehension later.
- **Lillard & Peterson 2011** (*Pediatrics*): 60 four-year-olds, 9 minutes of a fast cartoon (SpongeBob, a scene
  change every 11 s), a slow one (Caillou, every 34 s) or drawing; the fast group did worse on executive-function tasks
  right after; the design could not separate pace from fantastical content; lasting effects not measured. The script
  does not name the shows.
- **Not studied:** sources note there is no study isolating CoComelon itself (a 2026 study was reported, methods
  unclear), hence «համարյա ուսումնասիրված չեն».
- **WHO 2019:** no sedentary screen time under 1; at most 1 hour a day for 2-4 year olds (plus 3 h of activity).
  AAP: avoid screens except video chat under 18-24 months; 1 hour of high-quality programming at 2-5. Critics: "overly
  focuses on quantity ... fails to consider the content and context" (an Oxford researcher); the UK RCPCH said the
  data were too weak to set thresholds.
- **Tips** (beat 10): co-viewing and slower, story-driven programmes follow AAP guidance; "autoplay off" is practical
  advice, not a study claim.
