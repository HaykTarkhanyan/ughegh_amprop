The idea in one paragraph

Ancient Greek statues were not white: they were painted red, blue and gold (frame 0: a white original next to its tagged reconstruction). The paint left traces, sometimes visible, sometimes only under raking or UV light, and Egyptian blue, lit with visible light, gives off near-infrared light that a camera can see. The Peplos Kore on the Acropolis still had paint when it was dug up; today only dark patches remain. Why we thought otherwise: two thousand years washed the paint off, Renaissance sculptors copied the bare stone, white marble became the ideal and leftover paint was often cleaned away; in the 1930s the British Museum even scraped the Parthenon sculptures whiter with copper tools. Some of our own churches, bare stone to us today, were covered with frescoes inside; at Akhtamar they can still be seen. Today's painted copies look toy-like to many, and they are hypotheses: the colour is a fact, the details are not. The takeaway: our picture of Greece comes from what time left, and for centuries we admired a beauty that was damage.

Facts (checked)
  • Gods in Color (Bunte Götter): developed by Vinzenz Brinkmann with Raimund Wünsche of the Munich Glyptothek, first shown in Munich in 2003, then the Vatican and Copenhagen (Wikipedia); now the Liebieghaus Polychromy Research Project, Frankfurt.
  • Methods: UV or raking light through a slit reveals "colour shadows" (New York Review of Books); Egyptian blue "luminesces strongly in the near-infrared ... so that even microscopic remains can be detected" (Met Museum, Chroma glossary; Liebieghaus).
  • Peplos Kore (Acropolis Museum 679, about 530 BC): found in 1886 north-west of the Erechtheion; "traces of paint survived on the surfaces of the statue when she was found"; dark oxidised patches show where pigment was (Cambridge Museum of Classical Archaeology). Gilliéron's 1887 watercolours were the basis of early painted casts.
  • Cleaning: "until the nineteenth century at least, the tendency was to remove residual traces" (Cambridge). In 1937-38 Lord Duveen, funding the new gallery, had the Parthenon marbles cleaned with copper tools and carborundum to look whiter; the extent of damage is disputed (Wikipedia, 1937-1938 Elgin Marbles cleaning scandal; William St Clair; the British Museum's own report). The script says only what is agreed: scraped with copper tools to look whiter.
  • Criticism of the copies: visitors found them "tasteless" (The New Yorker, 2018, via NYRB); a Stanford art historian suggested the colours were overdone; the Cambridge museum calls its own painted kore "an attempt to imagine". Hence the doubt beat.
  • Left out: Winckelmann's "the whiter the statue, the more beautiful" (the wording was not confirmed); the modern political use of white marble.
