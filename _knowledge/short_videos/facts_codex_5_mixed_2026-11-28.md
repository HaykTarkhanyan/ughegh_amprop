# «Ուղեղ Ամպրոպ»: Fact sheets for six educational videos

Checked against sources available on **28 November 2026**. **High** confidence means strong documentary or research support; **medium** means a qualified interpretation; **low** means insufficient verification. Quotations below are brief exact excerpts or numeric entries. Calculations are identified separately.

## A1. Did the 1911 theft make the Mona Lisa world-famous?

**1. Verdict on the premise**

**Partly true; “only after” is too absolute.** The painting was already celebrated, particularly among artists, writers and educated visitors. The theft greatly expanded its popular recognition through international illustrated newspapers. Its subsequent fame also depended on reproductions, artistic appropriations and international exhibitions.

Accurate wording: **“The 1911 theft helped turn an already celebrated painting into a worldwide popular icon.”** This interpretation is supported by the Louvre and historian Donald Sassoon. [Louvre](https://www.louvre.fr/decouvrir/vie-du-musee/la-joconde-exposition-immersive); [Sassoon, 2001](https://academic.oup.com/hwj/article-abstract/51/1/1/706426).

*Suggested source line: Donald Sassoon, 2001; Musée du Louvre; Stéphane Rouault/INHA, 2024.*

**2. Key facts**

- **High — Already famous before the theft.** The Louvre describes its pre-theft audience precisely: “très célèbre auprès des amateurs de peinture et des gens cultivés”—very famous among painting enthusiasts and educated people. Calling it an unknown or neglected painting is wrong. [Louvre, “La Joconde, exposition immersive”](https://www.louvre.fr/decouvrir/vie-du-musee/la-joconde-exposition-immersive).

- **High — Fame had accumulated through several processes.** Sassoon’s scholarly account includes its royal ownership, public display in Paris, nineteenth-century literary reputation, theft, parody and foreign exhibitions. His description places the theft “at the peak of the boom of the illustrated press.” This supports a major contribution, not a single-cause explanation. [Donald Sassoon, 2001, abstract](https://academic.oup.com/hwj/article-abstract/51/1/1/706426).

- **High — Theft date and thief’s occupation.** It was stolen on **21 August 1911** by **Vincenzo Peruggia**, an Italian glazier who had worked at the Louvre. The museum’s account identifies “un vitrier qui avait travaillé au Louvre”; its educational chronology gives “21 août 1911.” “Former worker employed on museum glazing” is more precise than implying he was a curator or guard. [Louvre gallery history](https://musee.louvre.fr/decouvrir/le-palais/de-la-joconde-aux-noces-de-cana); [Louvre theft booklet](https://mini-site.louvre.fr/trimestriel/2021/Vol-de-La-Joconde/3/).

- **Medium — Mechanics of the theft.** The well-supported core is removal from display, separation from the protective assembly/frame, and escape from the building on its weekly closing day. A recent account says “he separated the portrait from its heavy frame.” Entry time, overnight concealment and accomplices vary between accounts; do not animate these as equally certain. [Smithsonian, 2026](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/andrew-lloyd-webber-says-hes-writing-a-new-musical-about-the-time-the-mona-lisa-vanished-without-a-trace-in-1911-180988559/).

- **High — Recovery in Florence in December 1913.** The Library of Congress chronology gives **12 December 1913**, with the painting “recovered in Florence, Italy.” Peruggia had approached art dealer **Alfredo Geri**; the Louvre confirms that an Italian dealer alerted the authorities. Use the month in narration if the hotel inspection/arrest sequence is unnecessary. [Library of Congress chronology](https://guides.loc.gov/chronicling-america-theft-mona-lisa); [Louvre](https://musee.louvre.fr/decouvrir/le-palais/de-la-joconde-aux-noces-de-cana).

- **High for his stated motive; medium for his actual motive.** Peruggia claimed patriotic restitution: he “had wanted to return the painting to its rightful home.” That is his explanation, not an established account of his inner motivation. A financial demand complicates the story: historian Charles Nicholl reports **“500,000 lire.”** Do not convert that sum into modern money without a separate economic analysis. [Library of Congress, 2022](https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2022/08/historic-thefts-the-mona-lisa-and-legal-quests-for-repatriation/); [Nicholl, 2002](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2002/mar/28/londonreviewofbooks).

- **High — International publicity was substantial.** The Louvre describes coverage by “la presse du monde entier” and widespread reproduction of the image. This supports an international media frenzy. It does **not** supply a verified count of newspaper articles or reproductions. [Louvre](https://www.louvre.fr/decouvrir/vie-du-musee/la-joconde-exposition-immersive).

- **High for the phenomenon; medium for crowd estimates.** Visitors did come to see the missing painting’s place. James Zug describes spectators coming “to stare at the empty wall.” His article says thousands, but supplies no attendance series; use **“crowds”**, not an exact number. A contemporary photograph independently documents the empty display position. [Zug, Smithsonian, 2011](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/stolen-how-the-mona-lisa-became-the-worlds-most-famous-painting-16406234/); [Commons photograph and provenance](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mona_Lisa_stolen-1911.jpg).

- **High — Apollinaire was arrested and imprisoned.** INHA’s account, based on his archival papers, states “Il est interpellé le 7”: **7 September 1911**. It dates his provisional release to the evening of **12 September**. The investigation connected him to **separate stolen Iberian statuettes** through Géry Pieret. [Stéphane Rouault, INHA, 2024](https://www.inha.fr/actualites/quand-la-joconde-rencontre-les-demoiselles-davignon/).

- **High — Picasso was questioned; imprisonment is not established here.** INHA describes Apollinaire’s confrontation with Picasso before the investigating judge: “Il se retrouve confronté à Picasso.” Prefer **“Apollinaire was arrested; Picasso was questioned.”** Zug’s popular account instead says police “arrested the artist Pablo Picasso”; I trust INHA’s archival reconstruction for this distinction. [INHA](https://www.inha.fr/actualites/quand-la-joconde-rencontre-les-demoiselles-davignon/); [conflicting Smithsonian account](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/stolen-how-the-mona-lisa-became-the-worlds-most-famous-painting-16406234/).

- **High — Duchamp’s appropriation dates to 1919.** For **L.H.O.O.Q.**, he altered a reproduction, not Leonardo’s painting. MoMA explains: “In 1919, Duchamp takes a reproduction of the Mona Lisa” and adds facial hair. Its contribution to the icon’s cultural afterlife is an interpretation supported by Sassoon. [MoMA](https://www.moma.org/audio/playlist/352/4918); [Sassoon](https://academic.oup.com/hwj/article-abstract/51/1/1/706426).

- **High — Later foreign exhibitions were major additional events.** The US exhibition was in **1963**: Washington’s public dates were **“January 9 - February 3, 1963”**, followed by New York. Tokyo National Museum documents a **“Mona Lisa exhibition in April 1974.”** These are verified dates; their separate contributions to fame cannot be quantified from these records. [National Gallery of Art](https://www.nga.gov/exhibitions/mona-lisa-leonardo-da-vinci); [Tokyo National Museum](https://www.tnm.jp/modules/r_free_page/index.php?id=154&lang=en).

**3. Retellings to avoid**

- **“Nobody knew the painting before 1911.”** Contradicted by the Louvre and Sassoon.
- **“The theft alone made it famous.”** Overstates a complex historical process.
- **“Picasso and Apollinaire were both jailed for stealing it.”** Conflates their different treatment and the separate statuette affair.
- **“Peruggia recovered something Napoleon had stolen.”** False. The Library of Congress explicitly says it “had not, in fact, been taken from Italy by Napoleon Bonaparte.” [LOC](https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2022/08/historic-thefts-the-mona-lisa-and-legal-quests-for-repatriation/).
- **The mastermind selling six forged Mona Lisas.** Zug identifies this later story as having “no supporting historical evidence.” Present it only as an unsupported tale, preferably omit it. [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/stolen-how-the-mona-lisa-became-the-worlds-most-famous-painting-16406234/).

**4. Best hook**

> “A theft helped make this smile a global icon. In 1911, the Mona Lisa’s absence became world news.”

**5. Showcase idea**

Remove the painting from a reconstruction of its display position; transform its outline into newspaper reproductions spreading across a map. Use **1911 → 1913**, followed by **1919, 1963 and 1974** as the extension showing its continuing cultural career. Newspaper multiplication should be schematic, without an invented circulation counter.

**6. Images**

Commons descriptions and licence notices checked; **high confidence in the listed metadata**:

- [**File:Mona Lisa stolen-1911.jpg**](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mona_Lisa_stolen-1911.jpg) — **Public domain**, tagged **PD-anon-expired**; unknown photographer, reproduced in *The Century Magazine*, February 1914.
- [**File:Mona Lisa, by Leonardo da Vinci, from C2RMF.jpg**](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mona_Lisa,_by_Leonardo_da_Vinci,_from_C2RMF.jpg) — **Public domain**, **PD-Art / PD-old-100-expired**; faithful reproduction of Leonardo’s painting.

**7. Armenian anchor**

**None found** that would strengthen this particular story without a forced connection.

**8. Open questions**

The exact escape reconstruction, involvement of accomplices and balance between patriotic and financial motives remain unsuitable for categorical narration. No defensible numerical measure of the painting’s worldwide recognition before versus after the theft was verified.

Even institutional summaries contain errors: the LOC’s 2022 blog says **22 August**, while its chronology and INHA give **21 August**. Use **21 August for the theft**; the disappearance was discovered the following day. Likewise, the Louvre’s simplified animation says Apollinaire was imprisoned for three days; prefer INHA’s documented **7–12 September** sequence. [LOC blog](https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2022/08/historic-thefts-the-mona-lisa-and-legal-quests-for-repatriation/); [LOC chronology](https://guides.loc.gov/chronicling-america-theft-mona-lisa); [Louvre animation transcript](https://api-www.louvre.fr/sites/default/files/2021-11/Transcription%20textuelle%20Joconde.pdf); [INHA](https://www.inha.fr/actualites/quand-la-joconde-rencontre-les-demoiselles-davignon/).

## K2. The oldest known leather shoe: Areni-1, Armenia

**1. Verdict on the premise**

**Supported with a category qualification:** **“the oldest known surviving leather shoe,”** or more cautiously **“the oldest known complete enclosed leather shoe.”** It is not the oldest surviving footwear of every kind, and it does not establish where shoes were invented. Guinness retains the category **“First leather shoe.”** [Guinness](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/first-leather-shoe).

*Suggested source line: Ron Pinhasi et al., PLOS ONE, 2010; Gregory Areshian et al., Antiquity, 2012.*

**2. Key facts**

- **High — Found in 2008 at Areni-1.** The History Museum of Armenia states: “Կաշվե այս ոտնամանը հայտնաբերվել է 2008-ին”—this leather shoe was discovered in 2008. The excavation location was Trench 3, in Armenia’s Vayots Dzor region. [Museum catalogue](https://historymuseum.am/collections_type/5500-%D5%A1%D5%B4%D5%B5%D5%A1-%D5%AF%D5%A1%D5%B7%D5%BE%D5%A5-%D5%B8%D5%BF%D5%B6%D5%A1%D5%B4%D5%A1%D5%B6/).

- **High — Approximately 5,500 years old, not an exact birthday.** Pinhasi et al.’s combined leather result is **“3627–3377 Cal BC”**, at **“95.4%”** probability. Use **“about 5,500 years old”** in speech and the calibrated range on screen. [Pinhasi et al., 2010, Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2882957/).

- **High — Three leather measurements.** Table 1 reports **“4725±32”** BP, Oxford **OxA-20581**; **“4708±32”** BP, **OxA-20582**; and **“4700±20”** BP, Irvine **UCIAMS-65186**. These are uncalibrated radiocarbon measurements, not calendar years ago. [Pinhasi et al., Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2882957/). Oxford defines BP using **“0 BP equivalent to 1950 AD.”** [Oxford ORAU](https://www.arch.ox.ac.uk/orau-methods-and-results).

- **High — Grass dating needs a correction to the abstract.** Grass sample **OxA-20583** measured **4810±31 BP**. The later site report reproduces its calibrated interval as **“3653–3524 BC (95.4%)”**, matching the original Table 2. Its interval overlaps the leather’s; they are not identical. [Areshian et al., 2012, Table 1](https://iae.am/uploads/about_files/dq1jkdes1vj3btpg1foru9in4.pdf).

- **High — Size and wearer’s sex are separate questions.** The measured length is **“24.5 cm”**, approximately European **“37.”** UCLA’s release gives the comparison as American **women’s size 7**, not men’s 7, and says the wearer could have been “male or female.” Prefer the measurement and EU size; conversions vary. [Pinhasi et al., Results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2882957/); [UCLA, 2010](https://international.ucla.edu/apc/article/115597).

- **High — One-piece construction.** UCLA describes a **“single piece of cowhide”** shaped around the foot. The paper identifies wear corresponding to the right foot. The construction is closer to a simple leather wrap than a modern shoe with a separately attached rigid sole. [UCLA](https://international.ucla.edu/apc/article/115597); [Pinhasi et al., Results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2882957/).

- **Medium — Cowhide identification.** The paper calls it **“processed cow-hide”**, but explicitly says taxonomic identification needs further analysis. The release’s unqualified cowhide wording is stronger than the analytical discussion. For strict narration: **“probably cowhide.”** [Pinhasi et al., Results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2882957/).

- **High — Leather lacing survives.** The reported eyelet-set counts are **“four and 15”**: four at the back and fifteen across the top. Say **sets/pairs of eyelets**, not fifteen individual holes. [Pinhasi et al., Results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2882957/).

- **High for stuffing; medium for its purpose.** Grass was inside the shoe. UCLA describes two possibilities: insulation or maintaining its shape, comparing the latter to a **“modern shoe tree.”** The paper favours shape maintenance/storage from the archaeological context; neither function was directly observed. [UCLA](https://international.ucla.edu/apc/article/115597); [Pinhasi et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2882957/).

- **High for conditions; medium for their individual causal contributions.** UCLA credits the cave’s **“stable, cool and dry conditions”** and a **“thick layer of sheep dung”** that sealed the deposits. Avoid inventing a temperature, humidity percentage or claim that dung chemically “tanned” the shoe. [UCLA](https://international.ucla.edu/apc/article/115597).

- **High — Older than Ötzi’s footwear; younger than some fibre sandals.** The shoe paper dates Ötzi to approximately **“3365–3118 Cal BC”**; its table differs by one year at the older endpoint. Guinness separately notes older **“sandals made from plant fibres.”** Do not call Areni the first footwear humans ever made. [Pinhasi et al., Discussion and Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2882957/); [Guinness](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/first-leather-shoe).

- **High — Museum location.** The shoe belongs to the **History Museum of Armenia, Yerevan**. Its official catalogue lists **“Ինվենտար # 3194”**. Armenia’s official tourism site also identifies it as **“currently on exhibit at the Armenian History Museum.”** [Museum catalogue](https://historymuseum.am/collections_type/5500-%D5%A1%D5%B4%D5%B5%D5%A1-%D5%AF%D5%A1%D5%B7%D5%BE%D5%A5-%D5%B8%D5%BF%D5%B6%D5%A1%D5%B4%D5%A1%D5%B6/); [official tourism site](https://armenia.travel/?v=1.0.27).

**3. Retellings to avoid**

- **“The world’s oldest shoe of any material.”** The category must specify leather/enclosed footwear.
- **“A woman’s shoe.”** Modern size comparisons do not identify the ancient wearer.
- **“US men’s size 7.”** The university release says **women’s** size 7.
- **“The grass was definitely a sock”** or **“definitely packaging.”** Both exceed the evidence.
- **“Armenians invented shoes here.”** Discovery location does not establish invention or the wearer’s ethnic identity.
- Exact comparisons with Stonehenge or pyramids without specifying which construction phase. They add avoidable chronological complications.

**4. Best hook**

> “This leather shoe survived about 5,500 years—in Armenia. Even its laces survived.”

**5. Showcase idea**

Reconstruct the **24.5 cm** shoe from a single leather outline, threading **four back eyelet pairs and fifteen top pairs**. Label the hide **“probably cowhide.”** Add the grass and surrounding sealed cave deposit as the extension explaining preservation. Use **3627–3377 BCE** as a range.

**6. Images**

Commons descriptions and licences checked; **high confidence in the metadata**:

- [**File:Chalcolithic leather shoe from Areni-1 cave.jpg**](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chalcolithic_leather_shoe_from_Areni-1_cave.jpg) — **CC BY 2.5**; credited to **Pinhasi R., Gasparian B., Areshian G., Zardaryan D., Smith A., et al.**, from the paper’s Figure 1.
- [**File:Areni-1 cave entrance.jpg**](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Areni-1_cave_entrance.jpg) — **CC BY 3.0**; **Serouj**. Note that the entrance image is BY, not BY-SA.

**7. Armenian anchor**

**Direct and strong.** The discovery site, Armenian excavation partners and museum are central to the topic. The museum identifies **Բորիս Գասպարյան, Ռոն Պինհասի and Գրիգոր Արեշյան** as co-directors. [Official museum catalogue](https://historymuseum.am/collections_type/5500-%D5%A1%D5%B4%D5%B5%D5%A1-%D5%AF%D5%A1%D5%B7%D5%BE%D5%A5-%D5%B8%D5%BF%D5%B6%D5%A1%D5%B4%D5%A1%D5%B6/).

**8. Open questions**

The wearer’s identity, sex and the stuffing’s purpose remain unknown. The original paper contains inconsistent dating prose: its abstract repeats the leather interval for the grass, while **Table 2** gives the grass an older, overlapping interval. Prefer the table and its reproduction in the 2012 report. The introduction also gives a contradictory narrow shoe interval. None should replace the combined leather result used above. No new calibration was performed for this fact sheet.

## K3. The oldest known winery: Areni-1, Armenia

**1. Verdict on the premise**

**Supported when “winery” means a relatively complete, integrated production installation.** It does not mean the oldest wine residue, the first fermented beverage or the invention of winemaking.

Accurate wording: **“Areni-1 preserves the oldest known relatively complete winemaking installation, dating to around 4000 BCE.”** Barnard’s own formulation was **“oldest relatively complete wine production facility.”** [UCLA, 2011](https://international.ucla.edu/asia/article/119445).

*Suggested source line: Hans Barnard et al., Journal of Archaeological Science, 2011; Patrick McGovern et al., PNAS, 2017.*

**2. Key facts**

- **High — Approximately six millennia old.** Barnard et al. associate the installation with a level dated **“4223–3790 cal. BCE.”** Their title and abstract use approximately **4000 BCE**. UCLA publicised roughly **4100 BCE**, conventionally **“6,100”** years old. These are different levels of rounding, not evidence of an exact production year. [Barnard et al., 2011, p. 979](https://www.arenicave.am/en/download-file/?file_id=1%2F1721319379738.pdf&module=publications); [UCLA](https://international.ucla.edu/asia/article/119445).

- **High for the documented installation; medium-high for the global superlative.** Barnard’s “relatively complete” qualification matters. The evidence combines pressing equipment, vessels, plant remains and chemistry. Armenia’s **2026 Tentative List submission**, hosted by UNESCO, still describes an **“integrated winemaking installation.”** That submission is Armenia’s statement, not an independent UNESCO scientific certification. [UCLA](https://international.ucla.edu/asia/article/119445); [Tentative List document](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6967/).

- **High — Packed-clay pressing platform.** The paper describes **“raised edges made of packed clay”**, sloping towards a jar. UCLA gives the approximate basin dimensions as **“3 feet by 3-and-a-half feet.”** It was not a mechanical screw press. [Barnard et al., p. 979](https://www.arenicave.am/en/download-file/?file_id=1%2F1721319379738.pdf&module=publications); [UCLA](https://international.ucla.edu/asia/article/119445).

- **High for layout; medium-high for function.** Juice could drain into a large jar interpreted as a **“karas”**, with additional jars nearby. Fermentation and storage are archaeological interpretations of the vessel arrangement. Avoid depicting every vessel’s use as directly proven. [Barnard et al., Materials and instrumentation](https://www.arenicave.am/en/download-file/?file_id=1%2F1721319379738.pdf&module=publications).

- **High — Actual grape remains were present.** The paper records **“grape seeds”**, desiccated grapes, rachises and skins near the installation. These finds support grape processing independently of the chemistry. [Barnard et al., p. 979](https://www.arenicave.am/en/download-file/?file_id=1%2F1721319379738.pdf&module=publications).

- **High — Two positive chemical samples.** The study’s abstract reports positive results for **two** Areni samples. Its method investigated **“malvidin”**, a pigment associated with red grapes and also other plants, including pomegranate. It did not recover an intact bottle of ancient wine. [Barnard et al., abstract and discussion](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0305440310004115).

- **High — The paper explicitly limits its inference.** Its conclusion says: **“Fermentation, although likely, can only be assumed.”** Malvidin supports grape-product identification; the production layout and pressed grape remains support the stronger wine interpretation. The 2026 nomination’s description of “conclusive biomarkers of grape wine” is stronger than the paper warrants; trust the analytical paper’s qualification. [Barnard et al., p. 983](https://www.arenicave.am/en/download-file/?file_id=1%2F1721319379738.pdf&module=publications); [contrasting nomination wording](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6967/).

- **High — Drinking vessels were reported.** UCLA lists **“a cup and drinking bowl.”** Its fuller description identifies an animal-horn cup and a complete clay drinking bowl. These support consumption-related activity, but not a particular drinking ceremony. [UCLA](https://international.ucla.edu/asia/article/119445).

- **High — Older evidence of grape wine exists elsewhere.** McGovern et al. report Georgian evidence dated approximately **“6000–5800 BC”**; earlier published evidence from **Hajji Firuz, Iran**, dates to **“5400–5000 BC.”** Thus approximately 8,000-year-old Georgian wine evidence and approximately 7,000-year-old Iranian evidence can coexist with Areni’s younger, more complete production installation. [McGovern et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5715782/).

- **Medium — A ceremonial role is plausible.** UCLA notes **“dozens of grave sites”** in the surrounding archaeological context and reports the excavators’ ceremonial interpretation. Burial association does not establish exactly who drank the wine, what rituals occurred or whether all production was ceremonial. [UCLA](https://international.ucla.edu/asia/article/119445).

**3. Retellings to avoid**

- **“The world’s first wine was made in Armenia 6,100 years ago.”** Confuses an installation with earlier evidence of wine.
- **“Malvidin proves alcoholic fermentation.”** Explicitly stronger than the paper’s conclusion.
- **“The scientists found wine still inside the jars.”** They studied residues and associated remains.
- **“They used today’s Areni Noir grape.”** No verified identification linking these ancient remains to that modern named cultivar was found.
- **“Noah’s winery.”** A biblical narrative is not archaeological identification.
- **“UNESCO proved it is the oldest winery.”** The cited document is a state-submitted Tentative List nomination.

**4. Best hook**

> “This Armenian cave preserved a winemaking installation about 6,100 years old. The remarkable part is the equipment surviving together.”

**5. Showcase idea**

Show the verified arrangement: rimmed clay platform → draining juice → buried receiving jar → neighbouring vessels. Overlay grape remains and the two positive residue samples as separate evidence layers. Label grape stomping **“reconstruction”**, and fermentation **“strongly inferred.”**

The extension is a three-point chronology: **Georgia, 6000–5800 BCE; Hajji Firuz, 5400–5000 BCE; Areni installation, around 4000 BCE**. Explain the different evidence categories.

**6. Images**

Commons descriptions and licences checked; **high confidence in the metadata**:

- [**File:Areni-1 cave entrance.jpg**](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Areni-1_cave_entrance.jpg) — **CC BY 3.0**, **Serouj**.
- [**File:Areni-1 Cave Interior.JPG**](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Areni-1_Cave_Interior.JPG) — **CC BY-SA 3.0**, **Kiwiodysee**. This is a general excavation view; do not label every visible feature as the wine press.

**7. Armenian anchor**

**High — A living regional connection is documented.** Armenia’s official tourism site describes Areni’s wine festival, held in **“Areni village.”** A Smithsonian-supported local heritage guide describes growers producing wine from the **“endemic Areni grape.”** These support a present-day connection, not proof of an unchanged cultivar or uninterrupted practice since the Chalcolithic. [Official tourism guide](https://armenia.travel/things-to-do/armenian-wine/); [Smithsonian My Armenia guide, “Momik Wines”](https://public-media.secure.si.edu/filer/43/6f/436fba46-12f1-444e-8d6d-43494dbdd4c3/experience_armenia_with_locals_guidebook_my_armenia_program.pdf).

**8. Open questions**

The ancient beverage’s alcohol content, recipe, flavour, grape cultivar and exact ceremonial use remain unknown. The precise dating of individual activities within the installation is less certain than the broad archaeological chronology. Prefer BCE dates over treating “6,100” as an exact age.

## B4. Why does time seem to speed up as we age?

**1. Verdict on the premise**

**A familiar experience with qualified research support, not a universal law.** Age differences are clearest when people look back over long periods, especially a decade. Much weaker differences appear in judgments about ordinary shorter periods. There is no established single explanation.

Accurate wording: **“As people grow older, past decades often seem to have passed faster—but this differs from how quickly an ordinary day feels.”** [Wittmann and Lehnhoff, 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16512313/); [Friedman and Janssen, 2010](https://dare.uva.nl/id/2efc78c5-3f24-4b63-ad44-abf3db3a85e3).

*Suggested source line: Wittmann & Lehnhoff, 2005; Friedman & Janssen, 2010; Avni-Babad & Ritov, 2003.*

**2. Key facts**

- **High — The widely cited 2005 study was a questionnaire study.** Wittmann and Lehnhoff studied **“499 subjects, ages 14 to 94 years.”** This measured reported experience, not the physical speed of time or a lifetime-long record of the same individuals. [Original abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16512313/).

- **High — Its age effects were limited.** The authors say effects accounted for **“at most 10% of the variance.”** That is variance in questionnaire responses—not a finding that older people experience time 10% faster. [Wittmann and Lehnhoff, 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16512313/).

- **High — A larger 2010 study found generally small age differences.** Friedman and Janssen surveyed **“1865”** adults aged **“16 to 80”** in two countries. The major exception concerned **“how fast the last 10 years had passed.”** Respondents across ages generally reported fast passage. [Original abstract, University of Amsterdam](https://dare.uva.nl/id/2efc78c5-3f24-4b63-ad44-abf3db3a85e3).

- **High — Decades differ from weeks, months and years.** The 2010 paper contrasts its decade result with questions about the next hour and the **“last week, last month, and last year.”** Do not generalise the decade effect into a claim that every hour or day accelerates steadily with age. [Friedman and Janssen, original paper](https://researchmap.jp/read0153473/published_papers/1402627/attachment_file.pdf).

- **High — Daily-life sampling provides an important countercheck.** Droit-Volet and Wearden’s 2015 study used **“8 alerts per day for 5 days.”** Its reported result was: **“Time experience was similar in the young and the old participants.”** This concerns immediate experience, not necessarily remembered decades. [Original study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0001691815000220).

- **High for historical attribution; low as an established explanatory law — Proportional theory.** **Paul Janet, 1877**, proposed a relationship between subjective time and age. Wearden’s translation/commentary describes the proposal as **“proportional to the age of the person making the judgement.”** The familiar arithmetic—one year is 10% of a ten-year-old’s life and 2% of a fifty-year-old’s—is correct; its psychological interpretation remains a theory. [John Wearden, online 2021; journal issue 2022](https://www.growkudos.com/publications/10.1163%25252F22134468-bja10044/reader).

- **Medium — Time pressure is one supported explanation.** Friedman and Janssen’s findings favour the **“experience of time pressure.”** That supports a contributor to reported speed, not proof that busyness completely explains aging or that the proportional account has been definitively refuted. [Friedman and Janssen, abstract](https://dare.uva.nl/id/2efc78c5-3f24-4b63-ad44-abf3db3a85e3).

- **High for experimental results; medium for explaining aging — Routine affects remembered duration.** Avni-Babad and Ritov conducted four experiments and two field studies. They report: **“the duration of the task was remembered as being shorter in routine conditions than in nonroutine ones.”** One prospective condition reversed the pattern. This supports distinguishing remembered duration from time experienced during an activity. [Original paper, 2003](https://pluto.huji.ac.il/~msiritov/AvniBabadRitov.pdf).

- **High as Hammond’s explanatory description; medium as a general mechanism — The holiday paradox.** Claudia Hammond describes a trip where **“time went fast while you were away”**, yet afterwards it feels like a long absence. She calls this the Holiday Paradox. It is a useful illustration of different judgments, not a measured formula connecting travel to extra perceived lifespan. [Hammond’s author interview, 2014](https://deborahkalbbooks.blogspot.com/2014/07/q-with-author-claudia-hammond.html).

- **High — The reminiscence bump is a robust memory finding, with qualifications.** Adults commonly retrieve disproportionate numbers of autobiographical memories from youth and early adulthood, often approximately **10–30 years**. Koppel and Rubin call it **“one of the most robust findings in autobiographical memory research.”** Their review also shows that cueing methods alter its timing. It does not independently prove why decades seem faster. [Koppel and Rubin, 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4850910/).

**3. Retellings to avoid**

- **“Science proves life feels twice as fast at forty as at twenty.”** No such universal multiplier follows from these studies.
- **“A year feels shorter because it is a smaller fraction of your life.”** Present this as Janet’s proposal, not settled causation.
- **“Your brain stores exactly fewer memories every year.”** No verified universal count or annual rate was found.
- **“All adults experience hours faster than children.”** Conflates different age comparisons and judgment types.
- **“Novelty slows time.”** Specify whether you mean immediate experience or retrospective duration. They can differ.
- **“The reminiscence bump proves young adulthood contained objectively more experiences.”** Recall depends on retrieval cues and other processes.

**4. Best hook**

> “A week can fly by—and still feel long when you remember it. That distinction also matters when we ask why the years seem faster.”

**5. Showcase idea**

Use two equal-duration timelines: **“during the experience”** and **“looking back.”** A varied trip gains distinct remembered landmarks; a routine period becomes visually compressed. Label the landmarks **schematic**, not measured memory counts.

As the extension, show Janet’s **10% versus 2%** arithmetic with a persistent **“historical theory—not a proven perceptual law”** caption.

**6. Images**

**None needed.** Original timelines and memory symbols will communicate the distinctions better than stock brain photographs.

**7. Armenian anchor**

**None found** in the research checked. An Armenian everyday example can illustrate the concept if clearly presented as an example, not local experimental evidence.

**8. Open questions**

There is no agreed quantitative model translating chronological age into subjective years. Cross-sectional surveys cannot fully separate aging from cohort differences, beliefs and retrospective reconstruction. How novelty, attention, emotion, time pressure and memory contribute to long-term judgments remains unresolved. The cited research does not establish a guaranteed intervention for making life feel longer.

## X2. How many times can you fold a sheet of paper?

**1. Verdict on the premise**

**“No more than seven times” is false as a universal claim.** The practical limit depends on thickness, dimensions, folding direction, material behaviour and available force. Gallivan’s achievement is well documented; the reported MIT demonstration requires a significant methodological qualification.

*Suggested source line: Britney Gallivan, 2002; Christopher S. Baird, 2012; Guinness World Records; NPR, 2011.*

**2. Key facts**

- **High — Seven is not a universal physical limit.** Physicist Christopher Baird writes: **“Nothing stops a piece of paper from being folded more than seven times if the paper is thin enough.”** This does not promise unlimited folding of an ordinary office sheet. [West Texas A&M University, 2012](https://www.wtamu.edu/~cbaird/sq/mobile/2012/12/07/what-stops-a-piece-of-paper-from-being-folded-more-than-seven-times/).

- **High — Gallivan folded paper twelve times on 27 January 2002.** Guinness lists **“12 total number”** and **“27 January 2002.”** These are successive folds in half, not twelve arbitrary creases. [Official record](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/494571-most-times-to-fold-a-piece-of-paper).

- **High — She used an exceptionally long strip.** Guinness gives **“4,000 ft (1,219 m; 0.75 miles)”** of tissue paper. The exact conversion of 4,000 feet is 1,219.2 metres; Guinness rounds it. Do not describe this as twelve folds of A4 printer paper. [Guinness](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/494571-most-times-to-fold-a-piece-of-paper).

- **High — Gallivan’s single-direction limiting-length formula is**
  
  \[
  \boxed{L=\frac{\pi t}{6}(2^n+4)(2^n-1)}
  \]
  
  Here \(L\) is minimum initial strip length, \(t\) material thickness in the same units, and \(n\) the number of half-folds **in one direction**. Guinness prints **“L=πt/6(2ⁿ+4)(2ⁿ-1)”** and attributes it to her book. It is not a universal formula for arbitrary origami. [Guinness](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/494571-most-times-to-fold-a-piece-of-paper).

- **High — The formula is independently documented in mathematics reporting.** Ivars Peterson explains its variables, including **“minimum possible length”**, and cites Gallivan’s *How to Fold Paper in Half Twelve Times*, published by the Historical Society of Pomona Valley. I verified the formula through these sources; I did not directly retrieve the original booklet. [Science News, 2004](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/folding-paper-half-twelve-times).

- **High — MythBusters achieved eleven with equipment and a huge assembled sheet.** NPR reports **“11 times”**, with equipment and changing fold directions. The programme’s official clip describes paper **“the size of a football field.”** Footage was made at NASA Ames in 2006; *Underwater Car*, containing the segment, aired in 2007. [NPR](https://www.kcur.org/2011-12-05/record-folders-54-000-feet-of-paper-13-folds-one-new-standard); [official MythBusters clip](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6EQeh2aK81Q); [NASA Ames newsletter, p. 4](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/163182main_06_11astrogram2.pdf); [episode release record](https://tv.apple.com/us/episode/underwater-car/umc.cmc.1fh6t8nk2e6kgpqozvtyey50b?showId=umc.cmc.3hro3r4qlr1x617h4ktsr7wf).

- **High that it was reported; medium as a directly comparable folding record — The MIT demonstration.** In December **2011**, **St. Mark’s School students**, led by **James Tanton**, reported thirteen folds using **“54,000 feet”** of toilet paper at MIT. Crucially, NPR describes **“first stacking the toilet paper into 64 layers”** and taping strips together; Tanton counted that preparation as six equivalent folds. Describe the method, not simply “MIT students beat the record.” [NPR’s contemporary report and interview](https://www.kcur.org/2011-12-05/record-folders-54-000-feet-of-paper-13-folds-one-new-standard).

- **High — Guinness still lists Gallivan’s twelve-fold record.** Its accessible record entry continues to identify her and twelve folds. That supports **“Guinness lists twelve”**; it does not establish that every thirteen-fold demonstration was impossible or that Guinness publicly adjudicated Tanton’s method. [Guinness record entry](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/494571-most-times-to-fold-a-piece-of-paper).

- **High — Ideal thickness doubles with each half-fold.** Baird gives **“2ⁿt.”** With an explicitly assumed initial thickness of **0.1 mm**, seven folds give **128 layers, 12.8 mm**; twelve give **4,096 layers, 409.6 mm = 40.96 cm**. These are calculations ignoring compression, gaps and bend geometry—not measurements of Gallivan’s actual stack. [Baird](https://www.wtamu.edu/~cbaird/sq/mobile/2012/12/07/what-stops-a-piece-of-paper-from-being-folded-more-than-seven-times/).

- **High — Forty-two ideal folds exceed the Moon’s mean distance.** NASA gives **“384,400 kilometers.”** Calculating \(0.1\text{ mm}\times2^{42}\) gives **439,804.6511104 km**; forty-one gives **219,902.3255552 km**. Thus forty-two is the first integer fold count exceeding that mean distance under this thickness assumption. This is mathematical extrapolation, not a feasible experiment. [NASA Moon facts](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/facts/).

**3. Retellings to avoid**

- **“Seven folds is a law.”** False.
- **“Gallivan folded an ordinary sheet twelve times.”** Omits the material and kilometre-scale length.
- **“MIT researchers folded one untouched roll thirteen times.”** Misidentifies the students and hides the assembly/layering method.
- **“You can actually fold office paper until it touches the Moon.”** The illustration ignores physical feasibility.
- **“Gallivan’s paper was 0.1 mm thick.”** That is the assumption used here for calculations, not a verified measurement of her material.

**4. Best hook**

> “Seven folds is no law of nature. A student folded paper twelve times—and worked out the geometry.”

**5. Showcase idea**

Start with thickness doubling and a visible layer counter: **1 → 2 → 4 → … → 128**. Then change scale to **4,096 layers** and finally **42 folds / approximately 439,805 km**. Keep **“ideal model; initial thickness 0.1 mm”** visible.

The extension explains why the real challenge is obtaining enough material to bend around the growing stack. If illustrating Gallivan’s formula numerically, **0.1 mm and twelve single-direction folds imply a minimum length of approximately 879.096 m**, calculated from her formula. This is not her measured material thickness or actual starting length.

**6. Images**

**None needed.** Original paper geometry and counters are sufficient.

**7. Armenian anchor**

**None found.**

**8. Open questions**

Exact MythBusters sheet dimensions are **not cleared for narration**. NASA’s contemporary report says **“170-feet-by-280-feet”** while also claiming ordinary letter-paper proportions; a library educational document says **“170 ft × 220 ft.”** NASA is primary for the filming, but its numbers conflict with its own proportion statement. Use the programme’s approximate football-field description unless the footage resolves the dimensions. [NASA, p. 4](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/163182main_06_11astrogram2.pdf); [conflicting library document](https://listlva.lib.va.us/scripts/wa.exe?A3=ind1308&B=--_004_2663A3C9D68B6A4E9FC0496049EDEB280D3C9A7CCOVMSGCESMBX03_&E=base64&L=VALIB&P=4197626&T=application%2Fpdf%3B+name%3D%22YouthServicesNotes20130826).

Different record rules and the St. Mark’s preparation prevent treating all reported counts as directly comparable.

## I6. How noise-cancelling headphones work

**1. Verdict on the premise**

**Correct with an idealisation made explicit.** Active noise cancellation uses microphones, processing/control circuitry and speakers to reduce unwanted sound pressure near the ears through destructive interference. “An inverted wave” describes the desired acoustic result; a real controller must compensate for timing and acoustic paths.

Accurate wording: **“The headphones generate sound that opposes some incoming noise at your ears.”** [Sen M. Kuo, Texas Instruments, 1996](https://www.ti.com/lit/an/spra042/spra042.pdf).

*Suggested source line: Paul Lueg, US patent 1936; Lawrence J. Fogel, US patent 1960; Sen M. Kuo, 1996; Byun et al., 2026.*

**2. Key facts**

- **High — Cancellation uses superposition.** Kuo’s ideal ANC relationship is **“y(n) = −d(n)”**: the cancelling contribution opposes the unwanted sound. The ideal derivation uses equal amplitude and **180°** phase opposition. Real headphones achieve partial, frequency-dependent reduction. [Texas Instruments application report, 1996, printed p. 13](https://www.ti.com/lit/an/spra042/spra042.pdf).

- **High — There is a microphone–controller–speaker chain.** Lo and McPherson describe **“built-in microphones”**, an ANC system and emission through headset speakers. The speaker creates an additional sound wave; the system does not extract a noise object from the air. [Lo and McPherson, 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3668193/).

- **High — Feedforward senses outside; feedback senses inside.** Analog Devices describes microphones monitoring **“sound outside the headphone”** and **“the sound inside the headphone.”** Feedforward uses an external reference; feedback monitors the remaining sound inside the earcup. Hybrid designs combine both. [Analog Devices, 2022](https://www.analog.com/en/signals/articles/bowers-wilkins-noise-canceling-headphones.html).

- **High — Accurate timing and the listening position matter.** An, Wu and Liu show that microphone and eardrum responses can differ. Their paper identifies delay as restricting **“NR bandwidth”** and discusses the **“residual noise signal inside the earcup.”** A waveform perfectly cancelled at one microphone is not automatically cancelled equally everywhere. [Original engineering study, 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/12/3/977).

- **High — Low-frequency sound is generally the strongest application.** The same study targets the **“low frequency band.”** At low frequencies, the microphone–ear separation is small relative to wavelength; higher frequencies make spatial and timing errors more consequential. Engine rumble is therefore a good illustrative target. [An, Wu and Liu, 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/12/3/977).

- **High — Steady noise is generally easier than speech or abrupt changing sounds.** Bose states that **“steady background sounds are usually easier to reduce than sudden or unpredictable ones.”** Voices change rapidly and contain a wide frequency range. ANC can reduce speech; saying it cannot cancel any voices is false. [Bose technical explanation](https://www.bose.com/stories/how-do-noise-cancelling-headphones-work).

- **High — Passive and active isolation are different mechanisms.** Physical seals, cushions and materials reduce transmitted sound without generating anti-noise. Fogel’s patent notes **“mechanical damping of the earphones alone”**, especially for higher-frequency noise. ANC complements this physical isolation, particularly at lower frequencies. [Fogel, US2966549A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US2966549A/en).

- **High for the stated range; medium as a general product expectation — A qualified attenuation example.** Lo and McPherson describe non-adaptive feedback systems allowing **“up to 20 dB”** attenuation **“below 700 Hz.”** This is an upper/example capability in the cited discussion, not an average reduction across all sound or all headphones. [Lo and McPherson, 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3668193/).

- **High — A newer measurement study also gives a bounded example.** Byun et al. tested two commercial models and report: **“The ANC function reduced the background white and pure-tone noise by up to 20 dB, with a greater reduction at low frequencies.”** Keep the **“up to,”** noise types and frequency dependence. No universal “typical ANC attenuation” number was verified. [Byun et al., Hearing Research, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41690185/).

- **High — Lueg’s patent dates explain the 1934/1936 discrepancy.** **US2043416A**, *Process of silencing sound oscillations*, lists German priority **“1933-01-27”**, US filing **“1934-03-08”**, and grant/publication **“1936-06-09.”** Thus 1934 is the US filing year; 1936 is the US patent grant year. [Patent record and original document](https://patents.google.com/patent/US2043416A/en).

- **High — Fogel’s headphone-related patent predates Bose’s work.** **Lawrence J. Fogel’s US2966549A**, *Apparatus for improving intelligence under high ambient noise levels*, was filed **“1954-04-02”** and granted **“1960-12-27.”** It explicitly concerns noise cancellation using headphones and microphones. Prefer these documented dates over the loose claim that he “invented all noise-cancelling headphones in 1950.” [Original patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/US2966549A/en).

- **High as documented company history; medium for the inspirational anecdote — Bose’s development.** Bose dates Amar Bose’s flight inspiration to **“April 19, 1978”**. Its history records **“two prototypes”** supplied for the **1986 Voyager** flight and a commercial aviation headset in **1989**. This is a development history, not the invention of destructive interference or ANC itself. [Bose’s 1970s history](https://timeline.bose.com/decade/the-seventies); [Bose’s 1980s history](https://timeline.bose.com/decade/the-eighties).

**3. Retellings to avoid**

- **“Two sounds always make silence.”** They must have the appropriate amplitude, phase and timing at the target location.
- **“The headphones simply invert everything the microphone hears.”** Omits filtering, acoustic paths and control.
- **“ANC works only on perfectly repetitive noise.”** Too strong; broadband noise can also be reduced.
- **“Voices cannot be cancelled.”** False; cancellation is often less effective, not categorically absent.
- **“Every ANC headset removes 20/30/40 dB.”** A number needs a model, frequency, fit and measurement definition.
- **“Bose invented noise cancellation in 1978.”** Contradicted by earlier patents.
- **“Bose’s 1989 headset was unquestionably the first commercial ANC headset anywhere.”** Bose makes that claim, but competing aviation development histories complicate the superlative. Sennheiser’s history dates NoiseGard development for Lufthansa to **1987**. Safer: **“Bose introduced its commercial aviation headset in 1989.”** [Sennheiser history](https://sennheiser.com.ua/en/about/history/17/); [Bose history](https://timeline.bose.com/decade/the-eighties).

**4. Best hook**

> “Your headphones fight noise by making more sound. The trick is making that sound oppose the noise at your ears.”

**5. Showcase idea**

Animate a headphone cross-section with incoming pressure variation and an opposing speaker contribution. At the eardrum, add the curves: ideal **180°** opposition produces a flat sum; imperfect matching leaves a residual. Label the flat result **“ideal case.”**

The extension moves the microphone outside and inside the cup to explain feedforward and feedback. If a numerical attenuation caption is used, write **“up to 20 dB in the cited test—not across every frequency.”**

**6. Images**

**None needed.** An original cross-section and waveform animation should be clearer than product photography.

**7. Armenian anchor**

**None found** in the technical and historical sources checked.

**8. Open questions**

A single representative attenuation figure cannot be supplied honestly without choosing a product and test protocol. Frequency, seal, microphone position and signal type change the result. Historical “first” claims also depend on whether one means a patent, prototype, aviation deployment or commercial product. The flight anecdote should be attributed to Bose’s account.
