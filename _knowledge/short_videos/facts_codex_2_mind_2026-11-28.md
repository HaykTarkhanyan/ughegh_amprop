# «Ուղեղ Ամպրոպ»: fact sheets for five short videos

**Confidence:** **high** = strongly supported for the stated claim; **medium** = credible evidence with important limits; **low** = unverified or weakly supported. Confidence in a published result does not mean it applies to everyone. Quotes below are short verbatim excerpts or exact numerical entries, with locations where useful.

## C4. Anchoring: a random wheel moves your guess

**1. Verdict on the premise**

**True as a group-level experimental finding.** Accurate wording: “An irrelevant number can pull people’s subsequent estimates toward it.” The famous **25% and 45% were group medians**, not changes measured within individual participants. Many Labs 1 supports anchoring, but **did not repeat the wheel experiment**.

**2. Key facts**

- **High — Original task and question.** Participants saw a wheel-generated number, compared it with the requested percentage, then estimated that percentage. The intended denominator was **UN member countries**: what percentage were African? The original wording is less explicit. Quote: “percentage of African countries in the United Nations.” [Tversky & Kahneman, 1974, p. 1128](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~mckenzie/TverskyKahneman1974Science.pdf).

- **High — Wheel manipulation.** The familiar account describes a wheel marked **0–100**, arranged to stop at **10 or 65**. A scholarly review of Kahneman’s retrospective explicitly says: “rigged by experimenters to stop only at either 10 or 65.” The short 1974 report itself gives less procedural detail. [Shleifer, 2012, reviewing *Thinking, Fast and Slow*](https://shleifer.scholars.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum10626/files/shleifer/files/kahneman_review_jel_final.pdf).

- **High — Original results.** The **10** condition produced a median estimate of **25%**; the **65** condition, **45%**. Exact numerical excerpts: “25 and 45”; “10 and 65.” The paper calls these *median estimates*. [Tversky & Kahneman, 1974, p. 1128](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~mckenzie/TverskyKahneman1974Science.pdf).

- **High — Many Labs 1’s scope.** The project collected **6,344 participants across 36 samples**. Its anchoring tasks came from **Jacowitz & Kahneman, 1995**, concerning distance, population, mountain height and births. Quote identifying the source: “Anchoring (Jacowitz & Kahneman, 1995).” **No wheel or African-UN question was used.** [Klein et al., 2014, abstract and task 3](https://web.stanford.edu/~knutson/jdm/klein14.pdf).

- **High — Many Labs anchoring results.** All four anchoring tasks showed significant effects in the predicted direction in every sample. Table 2’s corresponding proportion is **“1.00”** for each. Aggregate Cohen’s *d* entries were **“2.42”** for births, **“2.23”** for Everest, **“1.79”** for Chicago and **“1.17”** for distance. These are standardized effects, not percentage-point shifts. [Klein et al., 2014, Table 2](https://web.stanford.edu/~knutson/jdm/klein14.pdf).

- **High — Who actually threw the dice.** Study 3 recruited **52 junior legal professionals**, described as having early experience as judges. Exact excerpts: “52 legal experts”; “junior lawyers.” The often-repeated description of this particular sample as veteran judges is inaccurate. [Englich, Mussweiler & Strack, 2006, Study 3, p. 194](https://www.hf.uni-koeln.de/data/dppsenglich/File/PDFSStudien/PSPB_32.pdf).

- **High — Dice conditions.** Loaded dice generated totals of **3 or 9**, inserted as a hypothetical prosecutor’s sentencing demand. The paper specifies **“3 vs. 9 months on probation.”** These were experimental case judgments, not sentences imposed on actual defendants. [Englich et al., 2006, Study 3](https://www.hf.uni-koeln.de/data/dppsenglich/File/PDFSStudien/PSPB_32.pdf).

- **High for the reported result; medium for broad generalization — Dice outcomes.** Mean sentences were **7.81 months** after the high anchor and **5.28 months** after the low anchor; reported **p < .01**. Exact entries: “M = 7.81”; “M = 5.28.” [Englich et al., 2006, Study 3 results](https://www.hf.uni-koeln.de/data/dppsenglich/File/PDFSStudien/PSPB_32.pdf).

- **High — House-price study design.** Experiment 2 involved **47 agents and 54 students**, a property visit and information about the house. Listing-price anchors were **$119,900, $129,900, $139,900 and $149,900**. Exact table headings: “EXPERT SUBJECTS (n = 47)” and “AMATEUR SUBJECTS (n = 54).” [Northcraft & Neale, 1987, pp. 92–93](https://www.smallprojectsbureau.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/northcraft_neale.pdf).

- **High — Agents’ appraisals.** At the lowest and highest listing anchors, agents’ mean appraisals were **$114,204** and **$128,754**. Exact Table 3 cells: “114,204”; “128,754.” These were appraisals, not completed sale prices. [Northcraft & Neale, 1987, Table 3](https://www.smallprojectsbureau.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/northcraft_neale.pdf).

- **High — Students’ appraisals.** Corresponding student means were **$116,833** and **$144,454**. Exact Table 4 cells: “116,833”; “144,454.” A listing price can appear informative, so this is not identical to influence from an obviously irrelevant wheel. [Northcraft & Neale, 1987, Table 4](https://www.smallprojectsbureau.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/northcraft_neale.pdf).

- **High — Broader legal evidence, with a qualification.** A meta-analysis included **29 studies, 93 effect sizes and 8,549 participants**. It supported legal anchoring while reporting possible publication bias. Exact excerpts: “Twenty-nine studies”; “N = 8,549”; “possible publication bias.” [Bystranowski, Janik, Próchnicki & Skórska, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33734746/).

**3. Retellings to avoid**

- “The wheel made each person change their answer by 20 points.” The original comparison was between groups.
- “Many Labs replicated the wheel experiment.” It replicated different anchoring tasks.
- “Experienced judges gave real prisoners longer sentences after rolling dice.” This conflates participants, studies and hypothetical decisions.
- “Any number always controls your judgment.” The evidence supports conditional influences, not inevitability.
- “House prices increased by these amounts.” The measured outcomes were estimates.

**4. Best hook**

“A random wheel has nothing to do with the United Nations. Yet its number pulled people’s guesses.”

**5. Showcase idea**

Animate a wheel stopping at **10** and **65**, followed by two clearly labelled **group median** markers at **25%** and **45%**. For the extension, show the dice totals **3/9** beside hypothetical sentence means **5.28/7.81 months**. Avoid animating an individual’s opinion changing.

**Small source line:** “Tversky & Kahneman, 1974; Englich, Mussweiler & Strack, 2006.”

**6. Images**

**None needed.** Original wheel, dice and chart animation is sufficient.

**7. Armenian anchor**

**None found** in the sources checked.

**8. Open questions**

The 1974 account does not provide enough wheel-specific methodological detail for a modern replication audit. I did not verify a direct replication of the complete wheel-plus-UN procedure; **Many Labs 1 cannot fill that gap**.

## T13. Hanoi’s rat tails and the “cobra effect”

**1. Verdict on the premise**

**Hanoi is a defensible historical example; Delhi requires an anecdote label.** Say: “Hanoi’s rat-tail bounty rewarded a countable proxy that people could exploit.” Rat farming appears in Michael Vann’s scholarly account, but its source trail is less immediate than the campaign’s daily counts.

Goodhart’s law is a useful extension, provided its original monetary-policy context and Strathern’s later wording are distinguished.

**2. Key facts**

- **High — The 1902 campaign has an archival foundation.** Vann describes finding daily returns in French colonial archives and checking other records. Exact excerpt: “100-120 sheets of paper.” His interview places the returns in **1902**. [Vann interview, conducted by Marc Jason Gilbert, 2018](https://journals.gmu.edu/index.php/whc/article/download/3804/2200?inline=1).

- **High — Bounty rule.** Vann reports a **one-cent payment per tail**; whole bodies were considered an administrative burden. Exact excerpt: “a one-cent bounty.” Preserve the historical denomination; no modern currency conversion was verified. [Vann, 2003, p. 197](https://doi.org/10.1353/fch.2003.0027).

- **High for Vann’s documented account — Tail removal without killing.** Officials reportedly encountered living, tailless rats. Exact excerpt: “rats without tails.” Vann describes catchers removing tails and releasing the animals. [Vann, 2003, p. 197](https://doi.org/10.1353/fch.2003.0027).

- **Medium — Rat farming.** Vann reports inspectors discovering people **“actually raising rats to collect the bounty.”** This supports “Vann reports rat farming”; it does not establish its scale. [Vann, 2003, p. 197](https://doi.org/10.1353/fch.2003.0027).

- **High — Important source-date qualification.** The farming passage’s footnote 27 cites a municipal annual report covering **1930–1931**, rather than a surviving 1902 inspection report reproduced in the article. Exact bibliographic numbers: “1930”; “1931.” This warrants caution about the precise chronology. [Vann, 2003, note 27, p. 202](https://doi.org/10.1353/fch.2003.0027).

- **High as a recorded campaign count — A verified large daily number.** Vann gives **20,114 rats on 12 June 1902**. Exact numerical entry: “20,114.” His supporting note cites the **1902** destruction-of-animals dossier. Describe this as a reported tally, not an independently audited count or proof of population reduction. [Vann, 2003, p. 195 and note 22](https://doi.org/10.1353/fch.2003.0027).

- **Medium — Importing rats was another reported loophole.** Vann describes trafficking rats from elsewhere in northern Vietnam to claim Hanoi’s bounty. Exact excerpt: “a black market in trafficking rats.” No reliable quantity of imported rats was established. [Vann interview, 2018](https://journals.gmu.edu/index.php/whc/article/download/3804/2200?inline=1).

- **Low historical confidence — The familiar Delhi cobra sequence.** I found no primary document establishing the complete bounty → cobra breeding → cancellation → mass release story. An academic treatment explicitly states: **“this story’s historical validity is unclear.”** That is the appropriate evidential label. [Lucas & Fuller, 2018, p. 507](https://www.independent.org/wp-content/uploads/tir/2018/04/tir_22_4_02_lucas.pdf).

- **High — Naming the “cobra effect.”** Horst Siebert is credited with introducing the term in **2001**. Exact supporting sentence: “This term was coined by Horst Siebert (2001).” The term’s documented publication does not authenticate its historical anecdote. [Lucas & Fuller, 2018, footnote 1](https://www.independent.org/wp-content/uploads/tir/2018/04/tir_22_4_02_lucas.pdf).

- **High — Goodhart’s date needs two labels.** The underlying conference was in **July 1975**; the RBA lists the published proceedings as **1976**. Exact catalogue entries: “Sydney : Reserve Bank of Australia, 1976”; “Sydney, July 1975.” [Reserve Bank of Australia bibliography](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/1990/9013/conference-volumes.html).

- **High for Goodhart’s authenticated formulation; medium for verbatim identification with the first 1975 text.** Goodhart himself later quoted: **“any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes”**, directing readers to his **1984** collection. [Goodhart, 2013, p. 29](https://lelibellio.polytechnique.fr/uploads/volume9_num4/articles/goodharts_law.pdf). Parliamentary evidence gives a competing first formulation: **“Whenever a government seeks to rely on a previously observed statistical regularity for control purposes, that regularity will collapse”.** [Hood’s written evidence](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/153851/html/). For an exact on-screen quotation, I trust Goodhart’s own retrospective and label the wording accordingly.

- **High — Strathern’s popular version.** The concise sentence appears in her **1997** article, p. **308**: **“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.”** She discusses Hoskin’s treatment of Goodhart’s law. Do not attribute this wording verbatim to Goodhart in 1975. [Strathern, 1997](https://gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/1997-strathern.pdf).

**3. Retellings to avoid**

- Presenting Delhi’s cobra farms and mass release as established history.
- Claiming the Hanoi bounty increased rats by a verified percentage: no such before-and-after estimate was established.
- Turning a reported daily tally into “that many rats were certainly eliminated.”
- Saying rat tails regrew and were repeatedly redeemed. That is not the documented loophole.
- Treating every target as inevitably useless. These sources describe a vulnerability, not a universal mathematical rule.

**4. Best hook**

“Hanoi paid people for rat tails. Then officials began seeing rats still alive—without tails.”

**5. Showcase idea**

Show the intended chain: **tail submitted → one-cent payment → presumed dead rat**. Reveal the loophole: a tail reaches the counter while the living rat returns to the street. Label it “Historical reconstruction based on Vann.” Keep the rat-population counter unnumbered.

**Small source line:** “Michael G. Vann, 2003; Charles Goodhart, 1975/1984; Marilyn Strathern, 1997.”

**6. Images**

**None needed.** A schematic reconstruction avoids implying that unrelated archival photographs depict the incident.

**7. Armenian anchor**

**None found** in the sources checked.

**8. Open questions**

The exact start/end dates of the public bounty, the extent of farming, and the number of fraudulent claims remain unresolved here. The original colonial documents were not independently inspected. The precise first-edition wording of Goodhart’s 1975 statement also remains less secure than his later authenticated quotation.

## W6. Why you usually cannot tickle yourself

**1. Verdict on the premise**

**Broadly true, but “cannot” is too absolute.** Accurate wording: “Self-produced touch usually feels less ticklish because the nervous system predicts its sensory consequences.” These studies principally measured **ratings of tactile sensation**, not uncontrollable laughter.

**2. Key facts**

- **High — The basic phenomenon is attenuation.** The 1998 paper states: **“A self-produced tactile stimulus is perceived as less ticklish than the same stimulus generated externally.”** This is reduced sensation, not complete absence of touch. [Blakemore, Wolpert & Frith, 1998, abstract](https://www.nature.com/articles/nn1198_635).

- **High — The 1999 robot arrangement.** **16 participants** moved a device with the **left hand**, controlling another device stimulating the **right palm**. Exact excerpts: “16”; “via a second robot.” Do not swap this arrangement with the clinical experiment’s opposite hand configuration. [Blakemore, Frith & Wolpert, 1999, abstract and Methods](https://wolpertlab.neuroscience.columbia.edu/sites/default/files/content/papers/BlaFriWol99.pdf).

- **High — Delay conditions.** The manipulation added **100, 200 or 300 milliseconds**, alongside an undelayed self-produced condition. Exact Methods numbers: “100-, 200-, and 300-msec.” [Blakemore et al., 1999, p. 557](https://wolpertlab.neuroscience.columbia.edu/sites/default/files/content/papers/BlaFriWol99.pdf).

- **High — Spatial conditions.** Movement correspondence was rotated by **30°, 60° or 90°**, alongside the unrotated condition. Exact numerical excerpt: “30, 60, and 90°.” These were trajectory rotations, not angles at which a finger pressed the skin. [Blakemore et al., 1999, Methods](https://wolpertlab.neuroscience.columbia.edu/sites/default/files/content/papers/BlaFriWol99.pdf).

- **High — What delay changed.** Tickliness increased from **0 to 200 ms**; the reported analysis gives **“F = 24.93”** and **“p < 0.0005.”** Ratings at 200 ms, 300 ms and external stimulation did not differ significantly. That is not a formal demonstration of equivalence. [Blakemore et al., 1999, Results](https://wolpertlab.neuroscience.columbia.edu/sites/default/files/content/papers/BlaFriWol99.pdf).

- **High — What rotation changed.** Tickliness increased across **0–90°**; reported **“F = 9.37”**, **“p < 0.01.”** At 90°, ratings did not differ significantly from external stimulation. [Blakemore et al., 1999, Results](https://wolpertlab.neuroscience.columbia.edu/sites/default/files/content/papers/BlaFriWol99.pdf).

- **High — Other ratings require a distinction.** Self-produced stimulation was initially rated less intense and pleasant too; however, increasing delay/rotation did **not** produce corresponding significant graded relationships for those measures. Exact scale names: “Intense and Pleasant.” [Blakemore et al., 1999, Discussion](https://wolpertlab.neuroscience.columbia.edu/sites/default/files/content/papers/BlaFriWol99.pdf).

- **Medium–high — Prediction explanation.** A proposed *forward model* uses information about the motor command—an *efference copy*—to predict the resulting sensation. A good match permits attenuation. Exact excerpt: “An efference copy of the motor command”. This is an explanatory model supported by the experiments. [Blakemore, Wolpert & Frith, 2000, review](https://web.stanford.edu/~knutson/ans/blakemore02.pdf).

- **High for the imaging observation; medium for its mechanistic interpretation.** The 1998 fMRI study found greater somatosensory-cortex activity for externally produced touch. Exact excerpt: “More activity was found in somatosensory cortex”. Its cerebellar findings support a predictive contribution, rather than proving a single “tickle switch.” [Blakemore et al., 1998](https://www.nature.com/articles/nn1198_635).

- **High — Clinical sample.** The 2000 study included **23 people diagnosed with schizophrenia**, **18 with affective disorders**, and **15 controls**. Exact Methods entries: “23”; “18”; “15.” The symptomatic subgroup contained **“17”** patients. [Blakemore, Smith, Steel, Johnstone & Frith, 2000, pp. 1133–1134](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/4734/1/4734.pdf).

- **High for that study; medium for generalization — Clinical exception.** Patients with auditory hallucinations/passivity experiences showed reduced differentiation between self- and externally produced touch. The association was with symptoms **“rather than to the diagnosis of schizophrenia.”** This is not evidence that every person with schizophrenia can tickle themselves. [Blakemore et al., 2000, abstract](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/4734/1/4734.pdf).

**3. Retellings to avoid**

- “Your brain cancels all sensation from your own hand.”
- “A 200-ms delay makes everybody laugh.”
- “The cerebellum knows it is you and switches tickling off.”
- “Being able to tickle yourself diagnoses schizophrenia.”
- “Surprise alone explains everything.” The experiments manipulated action–sensation correspondence.

**4. Best hook**

“A robot can make your own touch feel ticklier—just by delaying it.”

**5. Showcase idea**

Animate the two-device setup. Compare **0, 100, 200 and 300 ms**, using a schematic tickliness indicator rather than invented rating values. Then rotate the movement mapping through **0°, 30°, 60° and 90°** while keeping palm stimulation aligned as in the experiment.

**Small source line:** “Blakemore, Frith & Wolpert, 1999; Blakemore et al., 2000.”

**6. Images**

**None needed.** An original apparatus diagram will be clearer.

**7. Armenian anchor**

**None found** in the sources checked.

**8. Open questions**

These experiments do not establish a universal delay threshold or explain every form of tickling and laughter. The clinical association should remain a narrowly described research finding.

## O2. Seeing faces in sockets and clouds: pareidolia

**1. Verdict on the premise**

**True.** Ordinary objects can evoke face perception and engage face-selective visual processing. Accurate wording: “Your visual system can briefly process a face-like object more like a face.” This does not mean it consciously believes the object is a person.

**2. Key facts**

- **High — Definition.** Face pareidolia means perceiving facial structure in an object that is not a face. Exact excerpt: “perceiving facial features on inanimate objects”. [Taubert, Wardle, Flessert, Leopold & Ungerleider, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5584612/).

- **High — Wardle’s comparison used matched objects.** The stimulus set contained **96 photographs**: **32 illusory faces, 32 matched objects and 32 human faces**. Exact numerical entries: “96”; “32”; “32”; “32.” [Wardle, Taubert, Teichmann & Baker, 2020, Results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7481186/).

- **High — Brain measurements were complementary.** The study used **21 fMRI participants** and **22 MEG participants**. Exact entries: “N = 21”; “N = 22.” fMRI supplied spatial information; MEG supplied timing. [Wardle et al., 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7481186/).

- **High — Face-selective regions responded.** Illusory faces were represented more like human faces than matched objects in the **FFA and OFA**. Exact excerpt: “face-selective FFA and OFA”. The result concerns a network, not one universal “face detector.” [Wardle et al., 2020, Discussion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7481186/).

- **High — Processing was rapid.** Category decoding was significant by **130 ms**, with an initial peak around **160 ms**. Exact Fig. 5 values: “130 ms”; “~160 ms.” These are neural-analysis timings, not measured moments of conscious recognition. [Wardle et al., 2020, Fig. 5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7481186/).

- **High — Representation changed rapidly too.** The abstract summarizes a shift toward ordinary-object representation within approximately **250 ms**; the detailed comparison contrasts **160 and 260 ms**. Exact numbers: “~250 ms”; “160”; “260.” These are approximate summaries of the same evolving response. [Wardle et al., 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7481186/).

- **High for behaviour; medium for subjective experience — Macaques.** Researchers recorded **five rhesus monkeys**. They preferentially viewed illusory-face objects and examined their apparent eyes/mouths. Exact excerpts: “five rhesus monkeys”; “eye”; “mouth”. This supports pareidolia-like perception without providing verbal reports. [Taubert et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5584612/).

- **High — Later neural evidence limits a simple cross-species story.** A 2025 single-neuron study found that macaque face-selective neurons **“exhibit minimal preference for pareidolia objects.”** This does not erase the behavioural finding; it cautions against claiming identical neural processing across species. [Koyano, Taubert, Robison, Waidmann & Leopold, 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S030100822400145X).

- **High — Viking’s original “face.”** Viking 1 photographed the feature on **25 July 1976**. NASA identified shadows and transmission errors as contributors to its appearance. Exact date: “July 25, 1976”; exact explanation: “Shadows in the rock formation give the illusion of a nose and mouth.” [NASA, original image description](https://science.nasa.gov/resource/geologic-face-on-mars-formation/).

- **High — The 1998 date discrepancy is distinguishable.** NASA’s detailed caption gives acquisition on **5 April 1998**, database retrieval on **6 April** and resolution **4.3 m/pixel**. Its summary incorrectly presents 6 April as acquisition. Exact entries: “5 April 1998”; “6 April 1998”; “4.3 meters”. Trust the detailed operational account over the short summary. [NASA, PIA01441](https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/mars-orbiter-camera-views-the-face-on-mars-calibrated-contrast-enhanced-filtered-brightness-inverted/).

- **High — The 2001 follow-up.** MGS obtained another targeted image on **8 April 2001 at 20:54 UTC**, published **24 May 2001**. NASA gives approximately **2 m/pixel** for this product. Exact entries: “20:54 UTC”; “about 2 meters”; “05/24/2001.” [NASA, PIA03225](https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/highest-resolution-view-of-face-on-mars/).

**3. Retellings to avoid**

- “The FFA mistakes every socket for a real person.”
- “You stop seeing the face after 250 ms.” Neural representation changing does not establish disappearance of the experience.
- “Monkeys reported seeing faces.”
- “NASA originally announced an alien monument.” Its original description identified a natural formation and an illusion.
- Presenting differently illuminated spacecraft images as a controlled before/after experiment.

**4. Best hook**

“That socket has no face. Your visual system finds one anyway.”

**5. Showcase idea**

Use a face-like object, then a slowed neural timeline marked **130 ms**, **160 ms** and **approximately 250 ms**. Label the diagram “schematic representation.” Extend to the real **1976, 1998 and 2001** Mars photographs, preserving their different illumination and image processing.

**Small source line:** “Wardle et al., 2020; Taubert et al., 2017; NASA/JPL/MSSS.”

**6. Images**

Both licences were checked on their Commons file pages:

- **[File:Martian face viking cropped.jpg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Martian_face_viking_cropped.jpg)** — **public domain, PD-USGov-NASA**. Author listed as Viking 1/NASA; date **25 July 1976**.
- **[File:Mars face.png](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mars_face.png)** — **public domain, PD-USGov-NASA**. Credit **NASA/JPL/Malin Space Science Systems**; date **8 April 2001**.

**7. Armenian anchor**

**None found** in the sources checked. A locally photographed face-like rock could illustrate the phenomenon, but would not establish a historical or scientific connection.

**8. Open questions**

The precise neural explanation across primate species remains unsettled. Timing depends on the task and analysis; **160 ms is not a universal face-recognition stopwatch**. For the Mars montage, use the image-specific NASA resolution rather than combining values from differently processed products.

## CX11. The blind spot: a hole in what you see

**1. Verdict on the premise**

**True, with a wording correction:** it is a gap in **light-sensitive sampling**, not an empty physical hole in the eyeball. Each eye has an optic-disc blind spot. Its corresponding location in visual space is roughly **15° toward the temple** from fixation.

**2. Key facts**

- **High — Anatomical cause.** At the optic disc, nerve fibres leave the eye and there are no photoreceptors to detect the image falling there. Exact excerpt: “a region devoid of photoreceptors and hence visual input.” [Meermeier et al., 2023, abstract](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10494866/).

- **High — Approximate visual-field location.** A research description places the blind spot approximately **15° temporally**. Exact excerpt: “centered about 15° temporally”. *Temporal* describes the direction in the visual field, toward the temple—not the optic disc’s retinal position. [Magnussen et al., 2007](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0042698906003932).

- **High as an approximation — Typical angular size.** The same paper uses approximately **5° wide × 7° high**. Exact numerical expression: “5° × 7°”. Treat this as a convenient approximate description, not everyone’s fixed dimensions. [Magnussen et al., 2007](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0042698906003932).

- **High — A modern measured sample illustrates variation.** In **12 right eyes**, mean horizontal centre was **15.53° ± 0.95°**, and horizontal width **5.30° ± 1.09°**; uncertainties are standard deviations. Exact entries: “N = 12”; “15.53°±0.95°”; “5.30°±1.09°”. [Meermeier et al., 2023, horizontal-border results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10494866/).

- **High — Filling-in works with one eye.** Observers can perceive a continuous surrounding pattern across the blind spot. Exact supporting sentence: **“The human natural blind spot is usually filled in based on the contextual information.”** [He & Davis, 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11248269/).

- **High — The other eye also helps.** During binocular viewing, the corresponding scene region can be represented by the fellow eye. Exact excerpt: “normal retinal and cortical representation of the corresponding region in the fellow eye.” Thus two-eye coverage and monocular filling-in are distinct explanations. [Magnussen et al., 2007](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0042698906003932).

- **High — Mariotte’s documented publication date.** Edme Mariotte’s *Nouvelle découverte touchant la veüe* appeared in **1668**. The Wellcome catalogue explicitly records **“Originally published: Paris : Frederic Leonard, 1668”.** “Published in 1668” is safer than assigning an exact discovery day. [Wellcome Collection](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/p5pavrqy).

- **High — Classic test orientation.** The Exploratorium’s arrangement has the **dot on the left, cross on the right**. Close the **right eye** and fixate the cross with the left. Exact instructions: “cross is on the right”; “Close your right eye”. [Exploratorium, *Blind Spot*](https://www.exploratorium.edu/snacks/blind-spot).

- **High — Movement and fixation matter.** Begin at roughly arm’s length, hold the card at eye level and slowly bring it closer while continuing to fixate the cross. Exact excerpt: “slowly bring the card toward your face.” The dot should disappear over a range, then reappear. [Exploratorium](https://www.exploratorium.edu/snacks/blind-spot).

- **High — A continuous-line variation demonstrates filling-in.** Draw a line through the dot and cross. Where the dot disappears, the line can appear unbroken. Exact excerpt: “the line appears to be continuous”. [Exploratorium](https://www.exploratorium.edu/snacks/blind-spot).

- **High — Octopus and squid lack this optic-disc blind spot.** Their retinal wiring does not require the same photoreceptor-free exit through the receiving surface. A university explanation states: **“Squids and octopuses happened to get it right, and have no blind spots.”** Use the anatomical claim, without its evaluative “get it right” phrasing. [University of Illinois](https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/785). A specialist anatomical review supports the structural distinction: **“Cephalopods have everted retinae”.** [Hanke & Kelber, 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6971404/).

**3. Retellings to avoid**

- “Your eye has a literal empty hole.”
- “The blind spot is at the centre of vision.”
- Giving its dimensions as identical for everyone.
- “You never notice it only because you have two eyes.” Filling-in also occurs monocularly.
- Claiming filling-in retrieves the object’s actual hidden details.
- “Octopuses can see everywhere.” Absence of this anatomical blind spot does not mean unlimited visual coverage.
- Repeating royal-court demonstrations as established history without checking them; historical accounts of those demonstrations conflict.

**4. Best hook**

“There is a patch in each eye that detects no light. You usually see no hole.”

**5. Showcase idea**

Keep the test image genuinely stationary:

1. Put a **dot on the left and cross on the right**, at the same height.
2. Tell viewers to **close the right eye** and keep the **left eye on the cross**.
3. Start around arm’s length and slowly move closer or farther.
4. Keep looking at the cross when the dot disappears; looking toward the dot defeats the test.
5. Add the continuous line as the extension.

Use a printed card or paused screen. Do not digitally erase the dot. Explain that the working distance varies with display size, spacing and the viewer.

**Small source line:** “Edme Mariotte, 1668; Meermeier et al., 2023; demonstration: Exploratorium.”

**8. Open questions**

Published dimensions differ because people, stimuli and boundary definitions differ. Use **approximately 15°**, **roughly 5° × 7°**, or explicitly identify the measured sample. **1668 is secure as the publication year**; a more precise discovery date was not established here.
