# «Ուղեղ Ամպրոպ» — Fact sheets for four videos

**Confidence:** high = directly supported; medium = plausible interpretation or limited evidence; low = speculative or unverified. Quoted support consists of short verbatim excerpts or exact numbers. Calculations are explicitly labelled. Findings from particular samples should retain their sample descriptions.

## L1. Why “mama” sounds alike in so many languages

**1. Verdict on the premise**

**Broadly true, with an important correction.** Similar nursery words recur across unrelated languages. Infant vocal production and adults’ interpretation of babbling offer a plausible explanation. However, “all these words arose independently” is too strong: individual words can also be inherited or borrowed.

Accurate wording: **“Similar sounds can produce similar nursery words independently. Their resemblance alone does not establish a common ancestor.”** Armenian *mayr* provides a useful contrast: its relationship to other Indo-European mother words is genuine.

**2. Key facts**

- **High — Jakobson’s essay originally appeared in 1960; 1971 is a reprint date.** The original contribution was in *Perspectives in Psychological Theory*, pp. 124–134. **Quoted bibliographic support:** “1960b” and “124–134.” The publisher identifies the later version as “1971, pp. 21–30.” Use **Roman Jakobson, 1960** on screen. Sources: [scholarly bibliography](https://benjamins.com/online/hl/articles/hl.22.3.04fal), [publisher’s reprint record](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9783110889598-002/html).

- **High — The historical sample counted terms, not 1,072 languages.** Jakobson reports Murdock’s material as **1,072 parental terms: 531 mother terms and 541 father terms**. **Quote:** “1,072 terms (531 for mother and 541 for father).” These denominators must accompany any percentage. Source: [Jakobson, reprint, p. 21](https://www.asau.ru/files/pdf/1817725.pdf).

- **High for the historical report — The verified nasal figures are approximately 55% for mother terms and 15% for father terms.** These combine the **M, N and ŋ classes**; they are not percentages specifically containing *mama*, or even specifically /m/. **Quoted numbers:** “55 per cent”; “15 per cent.” Source: [Jakobson, p. 26, reporting Murdock](https://www.asau.ru/files/pdf/1817725.pdf).

- **High for the qualitative finding; unverified for a precise stop percentage — Mother and father terms show different preferences.** Yale’s research summary records the supported hypothesis: **“Ma and Na sound classes will be used for the mother, while Pa and Ta sound classes will be used for the father.”** I did **not** verify an exact percentage for stops in father terms. In particular, subtracting 15% nasals from 100% does not establish 85% stops. Source: [Yale HRAF’s account of Murdock, 1959](https://hraf.yale.edu/ehc/documents/745).

- **Medium — Jakobson’s nursing explanation is a hypothesis, not a demonstrated universal developmental sequence.** He proposed that nursing-associated nasal vocalization could develop into an open-vowel nursery form, with adults helping assign meaning. **Quoted support:** “a slight nasal murmur.” Say “Jakobson proposed,” rather than “scientists proved babies say *mama* because they breastfeed.” Sources: [Jakobson’s essay](https://www.asau.ru/files/pdf/1817725.pdf), [scholarly discussion quoting the nursing passage](https://www.ibew.org.uk/dvarch/DV05903.pdf).

- **High for the observed sound association; medium for its causal interpretation — Babbling research supports recurring articulatory patterns.** MacNeilage and Davis found an association between **labial consonants and central vowels**, alongside other consonant–vowel preferences. **Quote:** “labial (lip) consonants with central vowels.” This supports a production-based explanation for *ma/ba*-like syllables; it does not prove why a particular language assigns one to “mother.” Source: [MacNeilage & Davis, 2000, original paper abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10775113/).

- **High — Nursery-word resemblance is weak evidence for linguistic ancestry.** Historical linguists explicitly discuss the **“non-genetic similarity among such terms cross-linguistically.”** Establishing relationship requires wider, systematic correspondences, not a matching parental word alone. Source: [Campbell & Poser, 2008, *Language Classification: History and Method*](https://ndl.ethernet.edu.et/bitstream/123456789/15040/1/174%20pdf.pdf).

- **High — Georgian supplies the requested counterexample.** **მამა, *mama*, means father; დედა, *deda*, means mother.** **Quoted entries:** “„დედა“ /deda/ ‘mother’” and “„მამა“ /mama/ ‘father’.” This directly disproves a universal *mama* → mother rule. Source: [Salome Omiadze, 2022, Tbilisi State University repository](https://dspace.tsu.ge/items/c16feb27-47a6-4dbf-bd02-6b1d0c21fbdf).

- **High — Armenian մայր, *mayr*, belongs to the inherited Indo-European mother-word family.** The University of Texas lexicon places it under **“mātér- ‘mother’”**, with the entry **“Armenian: mayr … mother.”** Thus Armenian *mayr*, Latin *māter* and English *mother* are not an example of mere accidental nursery-word resemblance. Source: [University of Texas Indo-European Lexicon](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/lex/master/1228).

- **High — Armenian հայր, *hayr*, is likewise inherited, despite its initial h.** Martirosyan explains: **“The PIE initial *p- yields Arm. h-”**, and compares *hayr* with Sanskrit *pitā*, Greek *patēr* and Latin *pater*. This is the kind of systematic sound correspondence that supports ancestry. Source: [Hrach Martirosyan, 2014, pp. 3–4](https://ling.hse.ru/data/2014/09/01/1313574129/Hrach%20Martirosyan%20-%20Handout.pdf).

- **High for usage; medium for origin — Armenian մամա has a documented etymological dispute.** Khachatryan records *mama* among regional mother terms: **“մէր, ադէ, մամա, ամայ.”** She reports Ačaṙyan’s Greek-loan explanation and Jahukyan’s preference for native nursery formations, allowing contact with Greek and Persian. Jahukyan’s qualification includes **“հնարավոր է որոշ տարածքներում խառնում”**—possible mixing in some regions. **Trust the conclusion “disputed,” rather than either categorical derivation:** the modern scholarly account explicitly preserves both explanations. Source: [Hasmik Khachatryan, 2014, printed pp. 37–39](https://language.sci.am/sites/default/files/book/hay_barbarhagitowtyan_khndirner.pdf).

**3. Retellings to avoid**

- “Every language calls its mother *mama*.” Georgian alone defeats that claim.
- “55% of languages say *mama*.” The reported figure concerns nasal sound classes among sampled **terms**.
- “85% of father words contain stops.” That number was not verified.
- “/m/ is every baby’s first consonant” or “*mama* is every baby’s first word.” Neither follows from the cited babbling work.
- “Similar parental words prove a single original human language.”
- The opposite overstatement: “Parental-word similarities never reflect inheritance or borrowing.”

**4. Best hook**

> “In Georgian, *mama* means ‘father.’ So why do similar baby sounds so often become words for parents?”

**5. Showcase idea**

Animate two bars labelled **“Murdock’s sampled terms, as reported by Jakobson”**: approximately **55% nasal classes for mother**, **15% for father**. Label the nasal group **m, n, ŋ**, rather than showing only *mama*.

Then reveal the Georgian labels as the extension: **მამა → father; დედა → mother**.

**Small source line:** “Murdock 1959; Jakobson 1960; MacNeilage & Davis 2000.”

**6. Images**

**None needed.** Original mouth diagrams, text labels and bars are sufficient.

**7. Armenian anchor**

Use **մայր / հայր** as securely inherited words, contrasted with nursery forms. The Armenian *mama* dispute is especially useful editorially: familiar sound does not by itself distinguish inheritance, fresh formation and borrowing.

The Georgian example provides a solid neighbouring-language connection.

**8. Open questions**

- I could verify Murdock’s nasal figures through Jakobson’s contemporaneous report, but could not directly audit Murdock’s original table. A precise father-stop percentage remains uncleared.
- The exact etymology of Armenian **պապա** was **not verified from an inspected authoritative dictionary entry**. Do not assign it a Russian, Greek or independently Armenian origin in this script.
- Jakobson’s complete nursing-to-word pathway remains a proposed explanation, not an established universal sequence.

## L4. Why some scripts run right to left

**1. Verdict on the premise**

**The direction facts are true; a single physical explanation is not established.** Script history and inherited conventions explain much of the distribution. Arabic, Hebrew and Syriac belong to the Phoenician–Aramaic historical network. **Thaana and N’Ko must not be placed on that same simple descent tree.**

Accurate wording: **“Writing direction is a historical convention. Related scripts often inherit it, but new scripts can adopt the same direction without sharing that ancestry.”**

**2. Key facts**

- **High — Arabic, Hebrew and Syriac run right to left.** Unicode states: **“Text in these scripts is written from right to left.”** These are scripts, each usable for more than one language; Arabic script and Arabic language are not interchangeable terms. Source: [Unicode 17, Chapter 9](https://unicode.org/versions/Unicode17.0.0/core-spec/chapter-9/).

- **High — Their ancestry needs intermediate branches.** Modern square Hebrew **“derives from the Imperial Aramaic script.”** Syriac likewise developed from Aramaic. For Arabic, Unicode calls Nabataean the **“precursor of the Arabic script.”** A responsible simplified diagram is Phoenician → Aramaic → Hebrew/Syriac, with Aramaic → Nabataean → Arabic. Sources: [Unicode, Chapter 9](https://unicode.org/versions/Unicode17.0.0/core-spec/chapter-9/), [Unicode, Chapter 10](https://unicode.org/versions/Unicode17.0.0/core-spec/chapter-10/).

- **High — Phoenician itself was right to left.** **Quote:** “Phoenician is written horizontally from right to left.” Its descendants did not all retain that convention, as Greek demonstrates. Source: [Unicode, §10.3](https://unicode.org/versions/Unicode17.0.0/core-spec/chapter-10/).

- **High — Thaana is right to left, but its construction is different.** It writes Dhivehi in the Maldives. Unicode states **“Thaana is written from right to left”** and explains that some letters were **“based on the Arabic-Indic digits.”** Arabic influence is real; calling Thaana simply another inherited Aramaic alphabet is misleading. Source: [Unicode, §13.1](https://unicode.org/versions/Unicode17.0.0/core-spec/chapter-13/).

- **High — N’Ko is a modern creation, not an ancient Aramaic descendant.** Unicode says it was **“devised by Solomana Kante in 1949”** for Manden/Manding languages, and is **“written from right to left.”** Source: [Unicode, §19.4](https://unicode.org/versions/Unicode17.0.0/core-spec/chapter-19/).

- **High — Early Greek could alternate direction between lines: boustrophedon.** A museum explanation says successive lines **“run in the opposite direction.”** Letters could also face the direction of reading. This was one historical practice, not a description of every early Greek inscription. Source: [National Museum of Ireland, Greek inscriptions](https://source.museum.ie/ga-IE/Collections-Research/Registration-Department/Registration-Blog/Greek-Letters%2C-Coins%2C-Troubleshooting-Adlib).

- **High — Left-to-right Greek became dominant during the fifth century BCE.** **Quote:** “It was during the fifth century BCE that the direction of left-to-right began to dominate.” This is a broad transition, not a universally enforced switch on a known day. Source: [Open University, “The direction of Greek writing”](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/mod/oucontent/view.php?id=157789&section=_unit6.1.7).

- **High — Decimal digit sequences in modern Arabic and Hebrew normally display the most significant digit on the left.** Unicode’s precise wording is **“the most-significant digit on the left.”** Say numbers are **written/displayed** left to right; this is not a claim that spoken number words always follow that order. Dates, separators and surrounding punctuation introduce further layout rules. Source: [Unicode’s bidirectional-text FAQ](https://www.unicode.org/faq/bidi.html).

- **High — The number rule is not universal across right-to-left scripts.** N’Ko numerals have the **“most significant digit at the rightmost side.”** Therefore, “letters go right to left but numbers always go left to right” is false when extended to every script. Source: [Unicode, §19.4, “Digits”](https://unicode.org/versions/Unicode17.0.0/core-spec/chapter-19/).

- **High — “About 405 CE” is an appropriate date for Mashtots’s alphabet.** Michael Pifer dates the invention **“around 404-6 CE.”** Unicode uses about 406. These are compatible approximations; **about 405** avoids pretending to have a securely exact year. Sources: [Pifer, 2021, *Encyclopaedia Iranica*](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/mesrop-mastoc%CA%BF/), [Unicode, Armenian](https://unicode.org/versions/Unicode17.0.0/core-spec/chapter-7/).

- **Medium for the historical explanation; high for direction — Greek influence plausibly explains Armenian’s left-to-right organization.** Kölligan and Kim explicitly list **“Greek alphabet most probably used as model”**, followed by **“writing from left to right.”** Pifer also connects Armenian’s Greek ordering and left-to-right separate letters with a western Christian visual configuration. This is scholarly reconstruction, not a surviving declaration by Mashtots explaining his decision. Sources: [Kölligan & Kim, slide 18](https://spw.uni-goettingen.de/projects/aig/doc/XCL-INT-001.pdf), [Pifer, 2021](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/mesrop-mastoc%CA%BF/).

**3. Retellings to avoid**

- **Low / unverified:** “Right-to-left writing began because the chisel was held in the left hand.”
- **Low / unverified:** “Greek switched direction because ink would otherwise smudge.” I found no primary historical evidence establishing either tool story as the explanation. **No supporting historical quotation was verified.**
- “All right-to-left scripts descend from Phoenician.”
- “Greek was always left to right,” or “every early Greek inscription was boustrophedon.”
- “All numbers in all right-to-left scripts run left to right.”
- “Mashtots chose the direction specifically to reject Syriac.” Greek influence is supportable; that personal motive was not verified.

**4. Best hook**

> “One sentence, two directions: Arabic and Hebrew text can run right to left while the digits inside it run left to right.”

**5. Showcase idea**

Show a correctly typeset Arabic or Hebrew line containing a **synthetic example number, 123**. Animate a large arrow right to left across the text, then a smaller arrow left to right across the digit sequence. Keep the hundreds digit on the left.

For the Armenian connection, end with Armenian’s left-to-right arrow and **“about 405 CE.”** Have a fluent reader check the final rendered line.

**Small source line:** “Unicode Standard 17.0; Pifer 2021; Kölligan & Kim, course materials.”

**6. Images**

**High — Commons licence checked:** [**File:Law Code Gortyn Louvre Ma703.jpg**](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Law_Code_Gortyn_Louvre_Ma703.jpg), photograph by **Jastrow**, **public domain**, released by the photographer.

**Licence quote:** “release this work into the public domain.” Useful as an inscription background; use accurate line-direction overlays, not invented translated content.

**7. Armenian anchor**

Mashtots’s alphabet is the strongest connection: **about 405 CE, left to right, with probable Greek influence**, despite the importance of right-to-left Syriac in the surrounding Christian literary world. Present influence as a scholarly inference, not a documented quotation from Mashtots.

**8. Open questions**

- The original reasons particular communities settled on particular directions are often unrecoverable.
- No decisive historical evidence for the chisel or smudging explanations was verified.
- Greek influence on Armenian is well supported structurally; Mashtots’s precise reasoning is not directly documented.

## A3. Why songs get stuck in your head — earworms

**1. Verdict on the premise**

**True phenomenon; incomplete explanation.** Research supports involuntary musical memory, identifiable triggers and associations with musical structure. It does not provide one settled mechanism explaining every earworm.

Accurate wording: **“An earworm is music recalled without deliberately choosing it. Recent exposure, memory cues and features of a tune can make it more likely.”**

For this video, the strongest core is involuntary retrieval; the extension is the limited evidence for interrupting it.

**2. Key facts**

- **High — The research term is involuntary musical imagery, INMI.** Williamson and colleagues describe a tune that enters the mind and **“repeats without conscious control.”** This is imagined music, not an external sound source. Source: [Williamson et al., 2012](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735611418553).

- **High for the sample — Liikkanen’s original article reports 89.2% experiencing INMI at least weekly.** The sample was **12,519 Finnish internet respondents**, using retrospective reports. **Quote:** “89.2% of participants reported experiencing this phenomenon at least once a week.” Do not convert this into a measured percentage of all humanity. The issue appeared in 2012; online publication was 2011. Source: [Liikkanen, 2012](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735611406578).

- **High — A later review reports a different figure and denominator.** Liikkanen and Jakubowski’s 2020 review gives **“92%”** and **“N = 11,910.”** **Editorial decision:** use the original article’s **89.2%, N = 12,519**, because that is the directly stated original result. I could not establish the reason for the review’s different denominator from the accessible material. Source: [Liikkanen & Jakubowski, 2020, frequency section](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-020-01750-7).

- **High for the qualitative pattern; unverified for a universal duration — The recurring material is commonly a short fragment, often centred on a chorus.** Primary research describes **“short segments or snippets of larger tunes”**; the review identifies a **“locus around the chorus.”** I did **not** verify a dependable universal “15–30 seconds” fragment length. Sources: [Williamson et al., 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0086170), [2020 review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-020-01750-7).

- **High for reported triggers — Recent/repeated exposure and memory cues recur in the evidence.** The original qualitative study distinguishes **“Music exposure, Memory triggers”**, alongside emotional and attention-related categories. Its full text separates recent from repeated exposure and describes associations with words, people and situations. These categories describe reports; they are not guaranteed triggers for everyone. Sources: [Williamson et al., 2012](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735611418553), [full-text transcription](https://paperzz.com/doc/8097625/how-do--earworms--start%3F---department-of-computing).

- **Medium — Stress is a reported association, not an established universal cause.** The study’s stress category concerns people **“experiencing anxiety or trauma”** at the time of an episode. This does not establish a stress-hormone mechanism or show that stress causes most earworms. Source: [Williamson et al., 2012, printed p. 271](https://paperzz.com/doc/8097625/how-do--earworms--start%3F---department-of-computing).

- **High for this study; medium for general prediction — “Sticky” songs combined familiar overall shapes with distinctive local movement and faster tempo.** Jakubowski and colleagues used **3,000 respondents**, then compared **100 frequently named tunes with 100 matched comparison tunes**. **Quoted findings:** “more common global melodic contours”; “less common average gradients between melodic turning points”; “faster average tempi.” “Unusual intervals” is an accessible simplification, but the actual finding concerns melodic slopes between turning points. Source: [Jakubowski et al., 2017, abstract](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/aca-aca0000090.pdf).

- **High — The most named songs were sample-specific, not a universal ranking.** Table 1 gives Lady Gaga’s *Bad Romance* **“33”** mentions, Kylie Minogue’s *Can’t Get You Out of My Head* **“24”**, and Journey’s *Don’t Stop Believin’* **“21”**. These are **counts, not percentages**. The paper appeared online in 2016 and in its journal issue in 2017. Source: [Jakubowski et al., Table 1, p. 126](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/aca-aca0000090.pdf).

- **High for the laboratory finding; medium as everyday advice — Chewing gum reduced reported musical intrusions.** Beaman, Powell and Rapley conducted **three experiments**. **Quote:** “chewing gum reduces both the number of voluntary and the number of involuntary—unwanted—musical thoughts.” The evidence supports an articulatory contribution; it does not establish a permanent cure or a universal success rate. Source: [Beaman et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25896521/).

- **Medium — Competing tasks can help, but “the harder the task, the better” is unsupported.** Hyman’s experiments found different intrusion rates across task types; the review reports **“less than 40%”** induction in the verbal-anagram task. However, Hyman’s abstract also states that **“overloading the cognitive systems with challenging activities increased intrusive song frequency.”** This supports cautious distraction advice, not a guaranteed anagram treatment. Sources: [Hyman et al., 2013](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/acp.2897/full), [2020 review’s task comparison](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-020-01750-7).

- **Medium for reported usefulness; low for a proven treatment — Listening to the whole song is a self-reported strategy.** Participants described trying **“to listen to the whole tune.”** The study generated hypotheses for future controlled tests; it did not establish a reliable cure rate. Source: [Williamson et al., 2014, “Engagement”](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0086170).

- **High that the strong explanation is unproven — The Zeigarnik account is not an established explanation of earworm onset.** The primary discussion reports that incomplete exposure was **“not a significant trigger”** compared with hearing complete songs. The possibility that incompleteness contributes to later repetition remains open. Say “one proposed explanation,” not “your brain repeats the song because it needs the ending.” Sources: [Williamson et al., 2014, discussing Hyman’s experiment](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0086170), [2020 review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-020-01750-7).

**3. Retellings to avoid**

- “90% of all people/adults get earworms weekly.” Preserve the original Finnish internet sample.
- “An earworm lasts 30 seconds.” Fragment length and the duration of a recurring experience are different measurements.
- “Only annoying songs get stuck.” Hyman’s research also found familiar, liked songs.
- “Scientists discovered the earworm formula.” The melodic findings are probabilistic associations.
- “Chewing gum cures earworms,” or “finish the song and it will definitely stop.”
- “The Zeigarnik effect explains earworms.” The key interrupted-song prediction was not supported.

**4. Best hook**

> “The music stopped. Your memory didn’t. An earworm is a tune that returns without you choosing it.”

**5. Showcase idea**

An original musical fragment enters a memory icon after listening, then returns through a loop. Label it **“illustration, not a brain scan.”**

For the numerical reveal, display **89.2%**, with **“12,519 Finnish internet respondents; at least weekly.”** For the extension, show gum interrupting some returns, with the caption **“reduced reports in laboratory experiments”**. Do not invent a percentage reduction.

**Small source line:** “Liikkanen 2012; Jakubowski et al. 2017; Beaman et al. 2015.”

**6. Images**

**None needed.** Original animation avoids implying that a stock brain image depicts the demonstrated mechanism.

**7. Armenian anchor**

**None found** that establishes an Armenian-specific prevalence, mechanism or remedy. An Armenian musical example could illustrate the experience, but should not be presented as a documented national earworm.

**8. Open questions**

- Why particular fragments repeat, and how repetition is maintained, remain incompletely explained.
- The **89.2% / 92%** reporting discrepancy requires further checking of the original analysis and exclusions.
- No universal fragment length was verified.
- The cited evidence does not establish reliable long-term, everyday cure rates for gum, anagrams or finishing the song.

## A5. Why there are 12 notes in an octave

**1. Verdict on the premise**

**Needs qualification:** an octave does not contain exactly twelve possible pitches by nature. Twelve is one useful historical arrangement. The near match between twelve pure fifths and seven octaves helps explain its mathematical usefulness; it is not a complete historical explanation.

Accurate wording: **“Why does a modern piano divide the octave into twelve steps?”** Keep separate **twelve pitch classes** and **twelve equal-tempered steps**.

**2. Key facts**

- **High — An ideal octave has frequency ratio 2:1.** Double the frequency to rise one octave. **Exact table support:** “octave … 2:1 1200.00.” The final number is its size in cents. Source: [Ross Duffin, 2006, Table 4](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.06.12.3/duffin_examples.pdf).

- **High — A pure perfect fifth has frequency ratio 3:2.** **Exact table support:** “perfect fifth … 3:2 701.96.” “Pure” matters: an equal-tempered fifth is slightly smaller. Source: [Duffin, Table 4](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.06.12.3/duffin_examples.pdf).

- **High — Twelve pure fifths nearly match seven octaves, but do not match exactly.** **Calculated here:**  
  \[
  (3/2)^{12}=129.746337890625,\qquad 2^7=128.
  \]
  Thus the proposed **129.75 versus 128** is correctly rounded. **Source equations:** “\((3/2)^{12}\approx129.7\)” and “\(2^7=128\).” Source: [John C. Baez, *The Mathematics of Tuning Systems*](https://math.ucr.edu/home/baez/tuning_book/).

- **High — The mismatch is the Pythagorean comma, approximately 23.46 cents.** Its exact ratio is  
  \[
  \frac{3^{12}}{2^{19}}=\frac{531441}{524288}.
  \]
  **Exact numerical support:** “531441:524288 23.46.” **Calculated here:** \(1200\log_2(531441/524288)=23.460010\ldots\) cents. Sources: [Duffin’s interval tables](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.06.12.3/duffin_examples.pdf), [Baez’s derivation](https://math.ucr.edu/home/baez/tuning_book/).

- **High — Twelve-tone equal temperament uses equal frequency ratios, not equal additions in hertz.** Each semitone multiplies frequency by  
  \[
  2^{1/12}=1.059463094\ldots
  \]
  **Quote:** “the semitone ratio of the twelfth root of two which is 1.059.” The additional digits above were calculated here. Source: [Joe Wolfe, *The Science of Music*, revised 2005, temperament section](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/~jw/reprints/SoMBooklet2005.pdf).

- **High — Equal temperament closes the circle by slightly narrowing fifths.** A seven-semitone fifth has ratio \(2^{7/12}=1.498307077\ldots\), rather than 1.5. **Source numerical support:** “\(2^{7/12}\approx1.49830707688\).” **Calculated here:** the fifth is **700 cents**, versus **701.955001… cents** pure: about **1.955 cents narrower**. Source: [Baez, equal-temperament discussion](https://math.ucr.edu/home/baez/tuning_book/).

- **High mathematically — Continued fractions explain why twelve is a good small approximation.** Approximating \(\log_2(3/2)\) gives the convergents **“3/5,” “7/12,” “24/41,” “31/53.”** Thus twelve divisions place a fifth near seven steps; fifty-three divisions place it near thirty-one. This mathematical reconstruction does not prove that ancient musicians used continued fractions. Source: [Baez, continued-fraction section](https://math.ucr.edu/home/baez/tuning_book/).

- **High — Seven, nineteen and fifty-three equal divisions are mathematically possible.** The table below is **calculated here**, using the closest integer step count and the formula \(1200[k/N-\log_2(3/2)]\). **Source formula support:** “\(\Delta=n/N-\log_2(3/2)\).” Twelve is a compact compromise; fifty-three approximates the pure fifth more closely. This compares **equal divisions**, not ordinary seven-note modes. Source: [Baez’s approximation framework](https://math.ucr.edu/home/baez/tuning_book/).

  | Equal divisions per octave | Steps nearest a pure fifth | Fifth size, cents | Error from pure fifth |
  |---:|---:|---:|---:|
  | 7 | 4 | 685.714 | −16.241 cents |
  | 12 | 7 | 700.000 | −1.955 cents |
  | 19 | 11 | 694.737 | −7.218 cents |
  | 53 | 31 | 701.887 | −0.068 cents |

- **High — Zhu Zaiyu published a mathematical derivation in 1584.** Lujia Sun’s historical study states: **“Zhu veröffentlichte im Jahr 1584 sein Neues Traktat der Stimmung”**, and explains its mathematical derivation of twelve-tone equal temperament. Credit this major contribution without claiming that one person invented every aspect of twelve-note music. Source: [Lujia Sun, 2025, GMTH Proceedings](https://www.gmth.de/proceedings/artikel/351.aspx).

- **Medium for precise dating; high for the contribution — Simon Stevin developed an early European mathematical treatment around 1600.** Rudolf Rasch’s specialist catalogue dates the manuscript **“ca. 1605”**, and says it was **“not published until 1884.”** An Oxford handbook instead places Stevin’s similar discovery **“one year before”** the relevant passage’s implied 1585 chronology—its wording actually says Zhu’s 1584 work was one year before Stevin’s. **Editorial decision:** prefer **“around 1600,”** with **“manuscript c.1605”** if needed, because the specialist catalogue explicitly dates the identified manuscript. Sources: [Rasch/Diapason catalogue](https://diapason.xentonic.org/ttl/ttl21.html), [Oxford handbook passage](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780190661977_A47780069/preview-9780190661977_A47780069.pdf), [St Andrews account of Stevin’s contribution](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Stevin/).

- **High — Arabic maqam practice cannot be reduced to twelve piano pitches or a universally exact 24-step grid.** Abu Shumays examines the **“hypothesis that Arabic scales are based on 24 equal divisions of the octave”**, then rejects it as an account of performance intonation. “Quarter tones” are useful terminology, but actual intonation depends on musical context and tradition. Source: [Sami Abu Shumays, 2009, pp. 1–3](https://maqamlessons.com/analysis/media/FuzzyBoundaries_MaqamIntonation2009.pdf).

- **High — Javanese gamelan supplies another clear counterexample.** **Quote:** “While slendro features five pitches distributed roughly equally within the octave, pelog has seven separated by clearly unequal step sizes.” Do not turn this into a claim that all Indonesian music uses one fixed five- or seven-tone tuning. Source: [Bill Alves, Harvey Mudd College](https://pages.hmc.edu/alves/pelog.html).

**3. Retellings to avoid**

- “Physics allows only twelve notes.”
- “Twelve fifths equal seven octaves.” The mismatch is the central point.
- “Equal temperament adds the same number of hertz at every step.”
- “Twelve-note music and twelve-tone equal temperament are the same invention.”
- “Pythagoras invented the modern piano scale,” or “Bach invented equal temperament.” Neither was established by the sources checked.
- “Nineteen is impossible,” or “fifty-three is mathematically worse.”
- “Arabic music uses exactly twenty-four equal pitches,” or “gamelan uses piano pitches with some keys removed.”

**4. Best hook**

> “Twelve perfect fifths almost return to the same note—but they miss. A modern piano makes each fifth slightly smaller so the circle closes.”

**5. Showcase idea**

Build two frequency ladders from a common reference:

- **12 pure fifths:** multiply by **3/2** each time.
- **7 octaves:** multiply by **2** each time.

Their endpoints land at **129.746…** and **128** times the starting frequency. Zoom into the **23.46-cent** gap. Then narrow each fifth by about **1.955 cents**, producing twelve equal-tempered fifths that close exactly across seven octaves.

Label any enlarged visual gap **“exaggerated for visibility.”**

**8. Open questions**

- The fifth–octave approximation explains mathematical usefulness; it does not by itself reconstruct why twelve prevailed historically.
- Stevin’s precise composition chronology varies across accounts. **Around 1600** is safer than an exact year.
- Claims of absolute priority for equal temperament require defining whether “first” means proposal, accurate calculation, publication or practical adoption.
- The original date of the quoted Komitas passage remains uncleared.
