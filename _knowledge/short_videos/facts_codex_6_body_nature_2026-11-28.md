# «Ուղեղ Ամպրոպ» — Research fact sheets

Checked **28 November 2026**. **High** confidence means strong support for the stated claim; **medium** means limited evidence or an interpretation; **low** means insufficient support. Quotes are exact short excerpts or numerical entries. The hooks, cautions and animation suggestions draw on the numbered facts.

## B1. Why we get goosebumps

**1. Verdict on the premise**

**True, with an important qualification:** goosebumps are produced by muscles controlled by sympathetic nerves. Raising fur provides insulation and can enlarge an animal’s apparent outline. Calling the human response “vestigial” is reasonable for those functions; calling the entire muscle–nerve system useless is inaccurate.

Accurate wording: **“Goosebumps are a fur-raising reflex we share with other mammals. The same muscle–nerve arrangement also has other biological functions.”**

**2. Key facts**

- **B1.1 — High: tiny smooth muscles produce the effect.** Arrector pili muscles attach to hair follicles; their contraction raises the hair and slightly elevates the surrounding skin. Exact excerpts: **“bundle of smooth muscle fibers”** and **“slightly raising the skin and making the hair stand erect.”** [Martel et al., *Anatomy, Hair Follicle*, updated 2024, Introduction](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470321/).

- **B1.2 — High: sympathetic nerves control these muscles.** This connects goosebumps with cold and emotional arousal. Exact excerpt: **“nerves from the sympathetic nervous system.”** [Robert Shmerling, Harvard Health, 2020, “What are goosebumps?”](https://www.health.harvard.edu/blog/wondering-about-goosebumps-of-course-you-are-2020080320688).

- **B1.3 — High: raised fur can retain insulating air.** This mechanism matters in a substantial coat of hair; do not imply that human goosebumps provide comparable insulation. Exact excerpt: **“traps air.”** [Shwartz et al., *Cell*, 2020, Introduction](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7540726/).

- **B1.4 — Medium: the human thermal response is conventionally described as vestigial.** This is an evolutionary interpretation supported by comparative anatomy. The primary paper states that in humans **“piloerection has lost its role in thermoregulation.”** That statement does not establish that every associated structure lacks a function. [Shwartz et al., 2020, Introduction](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7540726/).

- **B1.5 — High: cats can raise fur as part of a threatening or defensive display.** Raising the back and tail hair contributes to apparent size. Exact excerpt: **“adding to the impression of increased size.”** [Trudi Atkinson, Fellowship of Animal Behaviour Clinicians, *Cat Communication*, “Whole Body Signals”](https://fabclinicians.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/Cat-Communication-Article.pdf).

- **B1.6 — High: chimpanzee displays provide another documented example.** The Wisconsin National Primate Research Center describes a displaying male with erect hair: **“it makes an animal look larger to the nearby audience.”** This is a contextual display, not evidence that every frightened chimpanzee raises its hair. [UW–Madison, *Primate Behavior Slideshow*, page 29](https://primate.wisc.edu/primate-info-net/av-resources/slidesets/primate-behavior-table-of-contents/primate-behavior/primate-behavior-slideshow/).

- **B1.7 — High: porcupines raise their quills when threatened.** Accurate example; do not animate quills being shot. Exact excerpt: **“porcupine, which puffs out its quills when sensing danger.”** [Shmerling, Harvard Health, 2020](https://www.health.harvard.edu/blog/wondering-about-goosebumps-of-course-you-are-2020080320688).

- **B1.8 — High: musical chills are real, but a reported chill is not necessarily visible goosebumps.** Panksepp described **“a shivery, gooseflesh type of skin sensation.”** Sachs et al. describe chills as **“often accompanied by goose bumps”**—“often” matters. [Panksepp, *Music Perception*, 1995, abstract](https://www.jstor.org/stable/40285693); [Sachs et al., 2016, Introduction](https://doi.org/10.1093/scan/nsw009).

- **B1.9 — Medium: the frequently quoted Panksepp prevalence is 86%, within his sample.** A peer-reviewed systematic review reports **“86% of 828 respondents”** experiencing musical chills **“with some regularity.”** This verifies the review’s account, not a worldwide prevalence estimate. [De Fleurian & Pearce, *Chills in Music: A Systematic Review*, 2021, “Prevalence and Frequency”](https://www.marcus-pearce.com/assets/papers/deFleurianPearce2021b.pdf).

- **B1.10 — High for the reported survey; low as a universal percentage: terminology changes the answer.** The same review summarizes Sloboda’s sample: **“90% of a sample of 83 respondents”** reported shivers during the preceding five years, versus **“62% for goose pimples.”** These are different experiences and questions. [De Fleurian & Pearce, 2021, “Prevalence and Frequency”](https://www.marcus-pearce.com/assets/papers/deFleurianPearce2021b.pdf).

- **B1.11 — Medium: Sachs et al. found a preliminary brain-connectivity association.** They screened **237** respondents and compared **20** selected participants, **10** per group. Chills responders showed greater tract volume linking auditory and emotional/social processing regions. Exact excerpts: **“20 participants,” “10 in each group,” “increased tract volume.”** Selection deliberately created the two groups; this cannot establish that half of humanity experiences chills. [Sachs, Ellis, Schlaug & Loui, 2016, Methods and Figure 3](https://doi.org/10.1093/scan/nsw009).

- **B1.12 — High in mice: the 2020 finding concerned a stem-cell niche.** Sympathetic nerves signal to hair-follicle stem cells through norepinephrine; arrector pili muscles help maintain those nerve connections. Exact excerpts: **“regulate HFSCs through norepinephrine”** and **“APMs maintain sympathetic innervation to HFSCs.”** The experimental scope matters: NIH says researchers **“used skin samples from mice.”** [Shwartz et al., *Cell*, 2020, Summary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7540726/); [NIH, 2020](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/nih-research-matters/what-goosebumps-are).

**3. Retellings to avoid**

These cautions follow from B1.1–B1.12:

- “Goosebumps are completely useless.” The ancestral insulation explanation does not exhaust the system’s functions.
- “86% of people get visible goosebumps from music.” The figure concerns self-reported musical chills in a particular sample.
- “Only special, more emotional or more intelligent brains get musical chills.” Sachs’s small association study does not establish those claims.
- “Getting goosebumps makes human hair grow.” The 2020 experiments do not demonstrate that consequence in humans.
- “Music tricks your brain into believing you are cold.” This is an oversimplified explanation, not an established finding of these studies.

**4. Best hook**

> “A song can make your skin’s tiny muscles move. That is part of what happens when you get goosebumps.”

**5. Showcase idea**

Zoom from a bump into a skin cross-section: sympathetic nerve activates the arrector pili muscle → muscle contracts → hair rises. Then reveal the nerve’s nearby connection to stem cells, explicitly labelled **“Mouse experiments, 2020.”**

Suggested scope: muscle–nerve mechanism, plus the stem-cell extension. Avoid giving the schematic an invented contraction speed or suggesting instantaneous hair growth.

Small source line: **Shwartz et al., *Cell*, 2020 — mouse experiments.**

**6. Images**

**High — checked on Commons:** [**File:2003-09-17 Goose bumps.jpg**](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:2003-09-17_Goose_bumps.jpg), **Ildar Sagdejev**, available under **CC BY-SA 3.0**. Licence wording: **“Attribution-Share Alike 3.0 Unported.”** Provide attribution and identify alterations.

**7. Armenian anchor**

**High, lexical:** **«փշաքաղվել»** is supported by an Armenian dictionary entry. Its definitions include **«Մազերը բիզ-բիզ կանգնել»** and figuratively **«Սարսռալ, ահով լցվել»**. This provides a solid language connection without inventing an Armenian-specific physiological explanation. [Bararan.am, «փշաքաղվել»](https://bararan.am/փշաքաղվել).

**8. Open questions**

- A representative worldwide—or Armenian—percentage for musical chills is not established by the cited samples.
- I verified the **86% / 828** figure through the systematic review, not an independently inspected table in Panksepp’s full original article.
- The mouse stem-cell finding should not be presented as demonstrated human hair-growth physiology.

## B2. Why fingers wrinkle in water

**1. Verdict on the premise**

**True that ordinary immersion wrinkling involves an active nerve–vascular response.** Explaining it solely as skin absorbing water is inadequate. Explaining its evolutionary purpose as proven “rain tread” is also too strong.

Accurate wording: **“Water-induced fingertip wrinkling depends on sympathetic nerve function and constriction of blood vessels. Whether it reliably improves wet-object handling remains disputed.”**

**2. Key facts**

- **B2.1 — High: intact sympathetic innervation matters.** Wrinkling can disappear in denervated areas. Exact excerpt: **“cuts to sympathetic innervation to a finger abolish the wrinkling response to wetness.”** This is strong evidence against a purely passive soaking explanation. [Changizi et al., 2011, opening paragraph](https://www.changizi.com/uploads/8/3/4/4/83445868/pruney.pdf).

- **B2.2 — High: vasoconstriction is supported by blood-flow measurements.** Wilder-Smith and Chow measured circulation before and during wrinkling and concluded it is **“a function of digit pulp vasoconstriction.”** [Wilder-Smith & Chow, *Muscle & Nerve*, 2003, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12635117/).

- **B2.3 — High for the broad mechanism: reduced underlying volume pulls the surface into folds.** The physiological account describes **“Vasoconstriction, through loss of volume”** and a **“downward pull on the overlying skin.”** This does not mean water absorption has no effects on skin. [Wilder-Smith, *Clinical Autonomic Research*, 2004, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15095056/).

- **B2.4 — High: immersion wrinkling has clinical use.** Seamus O’Riain’s original 1973 report describes **“A simple objective test of innervation and regeneration”** using **“immersion in warm water.”** It is a clinical aid, not a definitive home diagnosis. [O’Riain, *BMJ*, 1973, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4755183/).

- **B2.5 — High for the reported testing conditions: onset can occur within a few minutes.** Cales and Weber reported: **“The optimal temperature for testing hands is 40 degrees C and skin wrinkling should occur in approximately 3.5 minutes.”** This is onset under their conditions, not a universal maximum-wrinkling time. [Cales & Weber, 1997, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9260639/).

- **B2.6 — High: 30 minutes is a common protocol, not a guaranteed biological endpoint.** Kareklas et al. used **“30 min.”** In a clinical study of **20 patients**, Grinbaum et al. reported **“24.5 min (± 11)”** to a positive result, with **“25% patients requiring 40 min.”** These clinical results should not be generalized as healthy-person onset times. [Kareklas et al., 2013, Methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3639753/); [Grinbaum et al., 2022, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35614228/).

- **B2.7 — Medium: “rain tread” began as a morphological/adaptive hypothesis.** Changizi and colleagues argued that wrinkles have **“signature properties of drainage networks.”** Their 2011 paper proposed a function; its morphology comparison did not itself experimentally establish improved handling. [Changizi, Weber, Kotecha & Palazzo, 2011, abstract and concluding paragraph](https://www.changizi.com/uploads/8/3/4/4/83445868/pruney.pdf).

- **B2.8 — Medium: Kareklas et al. found a wet-object handling advantage in 2013.** Their **20-person** experiment reported that **“submerged objects are handled more quickly with wrinkled fingers,”** with **“no difference to manipulating dry objects.”** This is a task-specific result. [Kareklas, Nettle & Smulders, *Biology Letters*, 2013, abstract and Methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3639753/).

- **B2.9 — High that the replication was negative; medium for generalization.** Haseleu et al. tested **“n = 40”** and found wrinkling **“did not improve dexterity in handling wet objects.”** They also found no measured improvement in touch sensitivity. This larger study makes a universal handling benefit untenable; it does not prove that wrinkles never help under any conditions. [Haseleu et al., *PLOS ONE*, 2014, abstract](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0084949).

- **B2.10 — Medium: later research found improved force efficiency using a different task.** Davis’s 2021 study analysed **516** participants and found wrinkles **“reduce the grip force needed to grip a wet object.”** However, **“participants allocated themselves”** to conditions. This supports a possible benefit, while neither resolving the earlier replication conflict nor proving evolutionary selection. [Nick Davis, *PLOS ONE*, 2021, Methods and Results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8294484/).

- **B2.11 — Medium: major wrinkle patterns can recur, based on a small 2025 study.** Laytin and German studied **“n = 3”**, used **“30 min”** immersion and repeated it a **“minimum of 24 h later.”** They found **“consistency of wrinkle morphology over time.”** Publication metadata says **“Available online 13 February 2025.”** This is not a 2023 paper or proof of perfectly identical lifelong patterns. [Laytin & German, 2025, pp. 1–2](https://www.newswise.com/pdf_docs/174679871746409_1-s2.0-S1751616125000517-main.pdf).

**Evidence balance:** trust the vascular mechanism more strongly than the adaptive explanation. The handling studies measured different tasks and produced conflicting results. Davis adds supportive evidence, but is not a direct replication of the marble-transfer experiments.

**3. Retellings to avoid**

From B2.1–B2.11:

- “Your fingertips simply swell like sponges.”
- “Scientists proved wrinkles evolved as tyre treads.”
- “Wrinkles improve grip by a fixed percentage.” No universal percentage is justified.
- “Everyone reaches maximum wrinkling at exactly 30 minutes.”
- “Wrinkles always repeat perfectly, like fingerprints.” The repeatability study had three participants and two measurement occasions.

**4. Best hook**

> “Your bath wrinkles involve your nerves. Beneath those folds, tiny blood vessels are constricting.”

**5. Showcase idea**

Show a fingertip cross-section: blood vessels narrow → underlying pulp volume decreases → the surface folds. Then overlay two illustrative wrinkle photographs, labelled **“Repeated immersion; preliminary study, n = 3.”**

If reproducing the 2025 protocol, use **30 minutes** and a repeat **at least 24 hours later**. Label the overlay as an illustration unless using the actual study images.

Small source line: **Wilder-Smith & Chow, 2003; Laytin & German, 2025.**

**6. Images**

**High — checked on Commons:** [**File:Wrinkly Fingers 001.JPG**](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Wrinkly_Fingers_001.JPG), **Sebastian Wallroth**, **CC BY 3.0**. Licence wording: **“Attribution 3.0 Unported.”**

**7. Armenian anchor**

**None found.**

**8. Open questions**

- **Maximum timing:** I could not verify a universal time to maximum wrinkling. Onset, a clinical positive result and maximal wrinkling are different endpoints.
- **Historical year — medium:** citations disagree between **1935** and **1936** for Lewis and Pickering. A [1977 *BMJ* paper](https://www.bmj.com/content/bmj/1/6060/552.2.full.pdf) cites **“1935”**; the [Wellcome catalogue](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/bn4nrydp) dates the publication **“1936.”** Haseleu also references 1936. I favour 1936 provisionally, but would use **“Lewis and Pickering, 1930s”** until checking the original issue.
- **Initial water sensing — medium:** the upstream mechanism is less settled than vasoconstriction. Wilder-Smith’s account explicitly begins **“Water probably initiates the wrinkling process.”** [2004 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15095056/).

## F3. Honey never goes bad

**1. Verdict on the premise**

**False as an unconditional statement.** Mature honey kept protected from moisture can resist microbial spoilage for a very long time. It can nevertheless ferment, and its flavour, colour and enzymes can change during storage.

Accurate wording: **“Honey is unusually resistant to spoilage when its water content stays low.”** The edible-Egyptian-tomb anecdote should not be used as established evidence.

**2. Key facts**

- **F3.1 — High: approximately 17–18% water is a reasonable typical figure, not a specification for every honey.** The classic USDA survey of **490 floral-honey samples** reported mean moisture **“17.2”**, with range **“13.4-22.9”** percent. [White et al.’s 1962 data, reproduced in USDA *Beekeeping in the United States*, 1967, Table 1](https://scientificbeekeeping.com/scibeeimages/USDA-1967-Beekeeping-in-the-United-States.pdf#page=63).

- **F3.2 — High: honey is a concentrated sugar solution.** FAO’s reproduction of the survey gives mean fructose **“38.2”** and glucose **“31.3”** percent by mass, alongside other sugars. High sugar concentration inhibits microbial growth through osmotic effects. Supporting primary excerpt: **“osmotic effect of the higher sugar concentration.”** [FAO, Table 2.6](https://www.fao.org/4/W0076e/w0076e04.htm); [White, Subers & Schepartz, 1963, p. 64](https://doi.org/10.1016/0006-3002(63)90359-7).

- **F3.3 — High: pH about 3.9 is a historical mean, not a universal value.** USDA’s table gives **“3.91”**, with range **“3.42-6.10.”** Use “typically acidic” rather than assigning every jar pH 3.9. [USDA, 1967, Table 1](https://scientificbeekeeping.com/scibeeimages/USDA-1967-Beekeeping-in-the-United-States.pdf#page=63).

- **F3.4 — High: glucose oxidase can generate hydrogen peroxide.** The original experimental paper states: **“Glucose oxidase, producing gluconic acid and hydrogen peroxide from glucose, is demonstrated in honey.”** This supports an antibacterial mechanism, not a claim of sterility. [White, Subers & Schepartz, 1963, p. 57](https://doi.org/10.1016/0006-3002(63)90359-7).

- **F3.5 — High: peroxide production needs qualification.** Glucose oxidase is **“basically inactive in concentrated normal honey.”** Its activity increases on dilution. Do not portray concentrated stored honey as continuously manufacturing large amounts of peroxide; low available water and acidity are central to its storage stability. [FAO, “Antibacterial activity”](https://www.fao.org/4/W0076e/w0076e04.htm).

- **F3.6 — High: honey can absorb moisture from its surroundings.** FAO describes its tendency to **“absorb and hold moisture.”** Consequently, storage conditions can change the very property that helps preserve it. [FAO, “Hygroscopicity”](https://www.fao.org/4/W0076e/w0076e04.htm).

- **F3.7 — High for the dependence; medium for any simple cutoff: fermentation risk rises with moisture.** USDA states: **“Above 19 percent water, honey can be expected to ferment.”** The surrounding discussion also identifies **“yeast count”** and **“temperature of storage.”** Thus **19–20% is a useful risk region, not an exact safe/unsafe switch**; sufficiently contaminated honey can ferment below it. [USDA, 1967, “Deterioration of Honey Quality”](https://scientificbeekeeping.com/scibeeimages/USDA-1967-Beekeeping-in-the-United-States.pdf#page=67).

- **F3.8 — High: crystallisation itself is not spoilage.** Glucose forms crystals in the concentrated solution. The extension guidance states: **“It is not an indicator of spoilage, impurity, age or quality.”** Crystallisation does not, by itself, certify safety either. [Kate Shumaker, Ohio State University Extension, 2016](https://ask.extension.org/kb/faq.php?id=299672).

- **F3.9 — High: resistance to microbial spoilage does not mean chemical permanence.** FAO identifies hydroxymethylfurfural, a fructose-degradation product, as **“formed during storage or during heating.”** A script should distinguish remaining usable from retaining original flavour and biochemical quality indefinitely. [FAO, “The composition of honey”](https://www.fao.org/4/W0076e/w0076e04.htm).

- **F3.10 — High that the familiar Egyptian examples fail to establish edibility.** Egyptologist F. Filce Leek reports that Tutankhamun’s labelled honey vessels were **“practically empty”** and that **“chemical tests were negative.”** A famous liquid described as honey from Yuya and Thuya’s tomb was analysed as **“Castor Oil.”** These records do not substantiate archaeologists eating preserved ancient honey. [Leek, *Bee World*, 1975, pp. 141–143](https://gwern.net/doc/history/1975-leek.pdf).

- **F3.11 — Medium for archaeological identification; low for the advertised “5,500 years/2003/world’s oldest” package.** Kvavadze’s original Kodiani study dates the tomb to **“27th–25th centuries B.C.”**, says it was **“excavated in August 2004”**, and reports pollen evidence that **“revealed that they contained honey.”** It does not establish edibility. These dates do not support 5,500 years for this tomb; the paper also does not verify a 2003 discovery date. Prefer the primary study over unsourced promotional retellings. [Kvavadze, *Paleontological Journal*, 2006, p. S595](https://repository.geologyscience.ru/server/api/core/bitstreams/4c0250a5-0af7-4d31-a93e-e90c792a1ea3/content).

- **F3.12 — High: infant botulism is a separate caveat.** Honey **“can contain spores of Clostridium botulinum.”** CDC’s guidance is: **“Do not give your child honey before 12 months.”** This concerns spores that can grow in an infant’s intestine; it does not require the honey itself to have spoiled. [USDA FSIS](https://ask.fsis.usda.gov/article/Can-I-feed-honey-to-my-baby); [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/infant-toddler-nutrition/foods-and-drinks/foods-and-drinks-to-avoid-or-limit.html).

**3. Retellings to avoid**

From F3.1–F3.12:

- “Honey never spoils, whatever you do to it.”
- “Archaeologists tasted perfectly edible honey from Tutankhamun’s tomb.”
- “Georgia’s 5,500-year-old honey proves unlimited shelf life.”
- “Crystals mean the honey has gone bad—or prove it is authentic.”
- “Honey kills every microorganism.”
- “Peroxide alone explains why a sealed jar keeps.”

**4. Best hook**

> “Honey’s long shelf life depends on keeping water out. Add enough water, and yeast can grow.”

**5. Showcase idea**

Animate a representative **100 g** portion using the rounded FAO values: **17.2 g water**, **38.2 g fructose**, **31.3 g glucose**, with the remainder labelled “other sugars and components.” Clearly mark these as **historical sample averages**.

Then let moisture enter: the solution becomes less concentrated and fermentation risk rises. Avoid an instantaneous “spoilage switch” at exactly 20%.

Small source line: **White et al., 1962; White, Subers & Schepartz, 1963.**

**6. Images**

**High — checked on Commons:** [**File:Crystallized honey with close-up.jpg**](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Crystallized_honey_with_close-up.jpg), **Zaereth**, **CC0 1.0**. Licence wording: **“CC0 1.0 Universal Public Domain Dedication.”**

**7. Armenian anchor**

**High for the study’s existence and scope:** Chirkova, Godocikova, Safranek and Majtan published a **2026** investigation of **“10 honey samples from four Armenian regions.”** It measured composition, glucose oxidase activity and antibacterial properties. The authors explicitly call it **“preliminary data rather than a comprehensive national survey.”** A suitable local connection is that these same properties are being measured in Armenian honey; avoid claims about all Armenian honey being superior or unusually permanent. [Chirkova et al., *International Journal of Food Science and Technology*, 2026](https://academic.oup.com/ijfst/article/61/1/vvag060/8537620).

**8. Open questions**

- **“Oldest honey”: not established here as a defensible global record.** A comparison must distinguish honey residues, mixed food residues and other bee products.
- I found no primary report establishing that the familiar Egyptian tomb honey was tasted and shown safe.
- The Georgian **5,500-year** figure may require a later redating or another documented find. I did not find the necessary evidence; do not silently replace the original paper’s chronology.
- There is no single moisture percentage that guarantees fermentation or guarantees its absence under every storage condition.

## Q3. Why planes leave white trails

**1. Verdict on the premise**

**True for ordinary exhaust contrails:** combustion produces water vapour; mixing with sufficiently cold air allows droplets to form and freeze into visible ice crystals. Persistence depends strongly on atmospheric moisture.

Accurate wording: **“A contrail is an ice cloud formed behind an aircraft under suitable atmospheric conditions. Some disappear quickly; others grow into spreading cirrus.”**

**2. Key facts**

- **Q3.1 — High: the visible trail consists principally of ice crystals formed from water vapour.** FAA describes **“water vapor released in the engine exhaust”** that condenses into particles which **“grow and freeze.”** The total exhaust also includes pollutants; identifying the cloud’s ice does not make all aircraft emissions harmless. [FAA, “Engine Exhaust Pollutants”](https://www.faa.gov/contrails).

- **Q3.2 — High as a typical condition, not an absolute threshold: around −40°C or colder.** NASA scientist Patrick Minnis describes formation as **“generally limited to temperatures below –40°C.”** “Generally” matters: formation depends on more than temperature alone. [Minnis, *Contrails*, NASA-hosted encyclopedia manuscript, p. 1](https://prod.satcorps.smce.nasa.gov/site/doc-library/208-Minnis.CONTRAILS.Ed2.pdf).

- **Q3.3 — High: the standard historical dates are Schmidt 1941 and Appleman 1953.** GLOBE’s history identifies **“Schmidt in Germany (1941) and Appleman in the United States (1953).”** Appleman’s original publisher record gives **“01 Jan 1953.”** The criterion considers mixing and saturation; Appleman’s abstract explicitly includes pressure, humidity and the exhaust’s water-to-heat ratio. [GLOBE history](https://www.globe.gov/web/s-cool/home/observation-and-reporting/contrails/history-of-contrails-and-contrail-research2); [Appleman, *BAMS*, 1953](https://journals.ametsoc.org/doi/pdf/10.1175/1520-0477-34.1.14).

- **Q3.4 — High: dry air can remove a trail quickly.** NASA states that in dry air contrails last **“just seconds or minutes.”** Their disappearance is not evidence that another, longer-lived trail contains a different secret substance. [NASA Earth Observatory, *The Evolution of a Contrail*, 2012](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/the-evolution-of-a-contrail-78154/).

- **Q3.5 — High: ice supersaturation allows persistence and growth.** The relevant humidity is relative humidity **with respect to ice**. Minnis describes growth while it **“remains above 100%”** and loss through **“sublimation”** below ice saturation. Initial formation and subsequent persistence have different saturation requirements. [Minnis, pp. 2–3](https://prod.satcorps.smce.nasa.gov/site/doc-library/208-Minnis.CONTRAILS.Ed2.pdf).

- **Q3.6 — High: some trails spread and last for hours.** NASA reports satellite-observed clusters **“lasting as long as 14 hours.”** This is an observed duration, not a universal maximum or the expected lifetime of every individual trail. [NASA Earth Observatory, 2012](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/the-evolution-of-a-contrail-78154/).

- **Q3.7 — High: contrails have competing radiative effects.** They **“reflect sunlight and trap infrared radiation.”** The balance depends on their properties and surroundings; persistent contrail cirrus has an estimated positive global net climate effect. [NASA Earth Observatory, 2012](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/the-evolution-of-a-contrail-78154/); [Lee et al., 2021, Table 2](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/45026/noaa_45026_DS1.pdf#page=31).

- **Q3.8 — High confidence in transcription; low confidence in the contrail magnitude, as assessed by the paper:** Lee et al.’s **2018 global annual-mean effective radiative forcing**, in **mW/m²**, was contrail cirrus **“57.4 (17, 98)”** and aviation CO₂ **“34.3 (28, 40).”** Parentheses are the **5th–95th percentile / 90% likelihood ranges**. Table 4a rates contrail-cirrus magnitude low confidence and CO₂ high confidence. These are energy-flux estimates, not emissions masses or per-flight comparisons. [Lee et al., *Atmospheric Environment*, 2021, Tables 2 and 4a](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/45026/noaa_45026_DS1.pdf#page=31).

- **Q3.9 — High for the survey result: 76 of 77 respondents reported no encountered evidence of secret large-scale spraying.** Exact excerpts: **“76 of the 77 scientists (98.7%)”** and **“had not encountered evidence of a SLAP.”** Participants included contrail specialists and atmospheric-deposition geochemists—not a representative poll of all scientists. The survey evaluated purported evidence; it was not a direct worldwide inspection of aircraft. [Shearer, West, Caldeira & Davis, *Environmental Research Letters*, 2016, abstract and Methods](https://chemtrails.info/wp-content/uploads/Shearer_2016_Environ._Res._Lett._11_084011.pdf).

- **Q3.10 — High for the observation; medium/contested for attribution: Travis et al.’s post-9/11 result concerned temperature range.** For **11–14 September 2001**, the original paper reported an **“increase of about 1.1 °C over normal 1971–2000 values.”** It attributed **“at least a portion of this anomaly”** to absent contrails. This was an increase in day–night range, not a 1.1°C increase in average temperature. [Travis, Carleton & Lauritsen, *Nature*, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1038/418601a); [indexed full-paper copy](https://bravenewclimate.com/files/travisd/pdf/jetcontrailsrecentresearch.pdf).

- **Q3.11 — High that a substantial critique exists: Hong et al. challenged that attribution.** Their analysis found the anomaly **“within the range of natural variability”** and associated it with **“change of low cloudiness.”** The abstract says it **“cannot be attributed to the absence of contrails.”** Treat the grounding as a weather-confounded observation, not a controlled demonstration. [Hong et al., *Geophysical Research Letters*, 2008](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2008GL036108).

- **Q3.12 — Medium: a later grounding study retained a possible smaller contrail contribution.** Sandhu and Baldini examined the **2010 UK volcanic-ash flight disruption**. They found anomalies **“largely attributable to weather system migration,”** while a contribution of **“up to +1 °C from contrail absence”** remained reconcilable with the observations. This is qualified compatibility, not an independently established causal temperature effect. [Sandhu & Baldini, *GRL*, 2018, abstract](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2018GL080899).

**Evidence balance:** the physics and climate assessment provide stronger grounds than the three-day grounding anecdote. Hong identifies a serious confound; Sandhu and Baldini preserve a possible contribution while acknowledging weather dominance. Neither warrants a clean “grounding proved X degrees” claim.

**3. Retellings to avoid**

From Q3.1–Q3.12:

- “Real contrails always disappear immediately.”
- “Persistent or spreading trails prove chemical spraying.”
- “−40°C is an exact universal switch.”
- “Contrails are harmless because they are water.”
- “Contrails cause precisely 1.7 times as much warming as aviation CO₂.” The central forcing estimates differ, but their uncertainties, timescales and interpretation matter.
- “After 9/11, America warmed by 1.1°C because planes stopped flying.” That confuses temperature range, mean temperature and disputed causation.

**4. Best hook**

> “A plane can leave a cloud that outlasts the flight. Whether it disappears or spreads depends on the air around it.”
